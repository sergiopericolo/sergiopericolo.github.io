Location models: p-median and p-center

Lecture 5 of Computational Methods (Carlo Filippi, University of Brescia). Where to open (or keep open) branches, warehouses, stores or emergency services? Location models answer with two ideas of "good service": the average distance of customers (p-median) and the distance of the worst-served customer (p-center). The two ideas conflict, and the final choice belongs to the decision-maker.

Roadmap:

  1. The PopBank case — from twelve branches to five.
  2. The p-median model — formulation and two-level structure.
  3. Local search for the p-median — the Teitz and Bart algorithm.
  4. The p-center model — minimizing the maximum distance.
  5. Balancing conflicting objectives — compromise solutions and the Pareto front.

The PopBank case

PopBank has a branch in each of the twelve villages of a rural area. The configuration is no longer competitive and the Board decides to cut the branches down to five. The area manager must choose which ones stay open.

On the map, nodes are villages, edges are roads, with distances and the demand for banking services of each village. Two more facts:

  • all branches are similar: the cost of keeping one does not depend on its location;
  • demand is moderate: congestion can be ignored.

A simple indicator of service level is the distance of the average customer from a branch. If total demand is given, minimizing the average distance is the same as minimizing the total distance, weighted by demand. The manager therefore looks for the five branches that minimize the total distance of customers. Note that branches and customers both sit in the villages.

The p-median model

  • Exactly \(p\) facilities (branches) must be located among \(m\) candidate sites (villages).
  • Facilities are identical and their cost does not depend on the site.
  • Each customer (village) is assigned to a single facility and has a known demand \(d_j\).
  • Each (facility, customer) pair has a known unit cost \(c_{ij}\) (the distance).

Sets and variables. \(I\) is the set of candidate sites, \(J\) that of customers. \(y_i = 1\) if a facility opens at site \(i\); \(x_{ij} = 1\) if customer \(j\) is served by the facility at \(i\). The cost of opening the \(p\) facilities is fixed and does not affect the optimization.

\[\min \sum_{i \in I} \sum_{j \in J} c_{ij} d_j x_{ij}\]

Constraint Meaning
\(\sum_{i \in I} y_i = p\) exactly \(p\) facilities are opened
\(\sum_{i \in I} x_{ij} = 1\) for every \(j \in J\) every customer is assigned to a facility
\(\sum_{j \in J} x_{ij} \le m \cdot y_i\) for every \(i \in I\) only open facilities serve customers
\(y_i \in \{0, 1\}\), \(x_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}\) binary variables

The third constraint is a "switch": if \(y_i = 0\) the right-hand side is 0 and no customer goes to \(i\); if \(y_i = 1\) the right-hand side is large enough to limit nothing.

Complexity and two-level structure

The p-median is NP-hard. Exact methods become prohibitive on large instances, especially with additional practical constraints, so heuristics are often used: greedy constructive methods, the Teitz and Bart heuristic, metaheuristics (tabu search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms…).

The problem has two levels:

  • the hard part is choosing which sites to open (the \(y\));
  • once the \(y\) are fixed, the rest is trivial: each customer goes to the closest open facility.

In formulas, if \(S \subseteq I\) is the set of open sites (\(\hat y_i = 1\) if \(i \in S\)), for each customer \(j\) let \(i_j \in S\) be the open site of minimum cost, \(c_{i_j j} = \min_{i \in S} c_{ij}\); then \(\hat x_{ij} = 1\) if \(i = i_j\) and 0 otherwise. The cost of a choice is

\[z(S) = \sum_{j \in J} d_j \min_{i \in S} c_{ij}\]

Local search for the p-median

We design a local search on the \(y\) variables only, that is on the sets \(S\) of \(p\) sites.

  • Initial solution: any set \(S\) of \(p\) sites.
  • Neighborhood \(N(S)\): all sets \(S' = S \setminus \{i\} \cup \{k\}\) with \(i \in S\) and \(k \in I \setminus S\): close an open site and open a closed one (a swap).
  • Better: \(S'\) is better than \(S\) if \(z(S') < z(S)\).

To reduce the work, each iteration explores \(N(S)\) only partially, fixing one candidate site \(k\) to open and trying it against all open sites.

The Teitz and Bart algorithm

Each site carries a label \(\ell\): \(1\) if open, \(0\) if closed and still to try, \(-1\) if closed and already tried without success.

  1. Initialization: \(S\) is any set of \(p\) sites; compute \(z(S)\) and \(\bar S = I \setminus S\); \(\ell(i) = 1\) for \(i \in S\), \(\ell(i) = 0\) for \(i \in \bar S\).
  2. Termination: if \(\ell(k) = -1\) for every \(k \in \bar S\), return \(S\) and \(z(S)\).
  3. Savings: choose \(k \in \bar S\) with \(\ell(k) = 0\) and, for every \(i \in S\), compute \(s_{ik} = z(S) - z(S \setminus \{i\} \cup \{k\})\); let \(s_{i^* k}\) be the maximum.
  4. Iteration: if \(s_{i^* k} \le 0\) set \(\ell(k) = -1\). Otherwise swap: \(S = S \setminus \{i^*\} \cup \{k\}\), update the labels (\(\ell = 1\) on open sites, \(\ell = 0\) on closed ones, \(\ell(i^*) = -1\) for the site just closed) and go back to step 2.

The key point: after every successful swap all closed sites become "to try" again, because with the new \(S\) a swap that was useless before may now pay off.

An example. Five candidate sites, six customers (unit demand), \(p = 2\), starting from \(S = \{1, 2\}\). The cost matrix \(c_{ij}\) is in the widget: follow the algorithm step by step. In bold, the cost each customer pays to its nearest open site; highlighted, the site \(k\) being tried.

[[7, 7, 14, 3, 4, 8], [17, 16, 12, 11, 7, 15], [8, 9, 15, 1, 5, 7], [11, 6, 11, 13, 11, 17], [19, 14, 3, 17, 12, 23]]

The path: \(\{1, 2\}\) (\(z = 41\)) → \(\{1, 3\}\) (40) → \(\{3, 4\}\) (38) → \(\{3, 5\}\) (33) → \(\{1, 5\}\) (32). After the last swap no closed site improves: it is a local optimum, and here it coincides with the global optimum found by trying all 10 pairs.

The p-center model

PopBank's p-median solution has an average distance of 7.8 km. But a Board member objects: "Is it fair? On average a client is 7.8 km from a branch, but village 3 is 28 km from the closest one!" There is large variability: some customers have a branch right under their house, others must travel 28 km.

The President proposes: "What if we minimize the maximum distance of a village from a branch? Would the average distance increase?"

The new objective is a minimax:

\[\min \max_{j \in J} \sum_{i \in I} c_{ij} x_{ij}\]

(\(\sum_i c_{ij} x_{ij}\) is the distance of customer \(j\) from its facility). To write it in linear form, add a variable \(D_{\max}\) and require it to lie above every distance:

\[\min D_{\max} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{i \in I} c_{ij} x_{ij} \le D_{\max} \quad (j \in J)\]

plus the same p-median constraints on \(y\) and \(x\).

Minimizing \(D_{\max}\), which must exceed every distance, pushes it exactly onto the largest distance. This is the p-center.

Solved with a solver (AMPL), on PopBank the maximum distance drops from 28 to 19 km, but the average distance rises from 7.8 to 9.9 km: the p-center ignores the total distance.

Balancing conflicting objectives

The President: "The new solution is more satisfying: our branches can be reached in under 20 minutes from any village, and an average travel time going from 7.8 to 9.9 seems acceptable. But before deciding I would like to know whether there is a comparable solution with a shorter total distance."

There are two conflicting objectives: total (or average) distance and maximum distance. The p-median optimizes the first and neglects the second; the p-center does the opposite. A compromise solution balances the two by minimizing a weighted average:

\[\min \alpha \cdot D_{\max} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \sum_{i \in I} \sum_{j \in J} c_{ij} d_j x_{ij}, \qquad 0 \le \alpha \le 1\]

  • \(\alpha\) close to 0: the p-median objective matters most;
  • \(\alpha\) close to 1: the p-center objective matters most.

The solutions found by the area manager:

\(\alpha\) Max distance Total distance
0 28 1444
0.1 28 1444
0.5 22 1447
0.9 19 1465
1 19 1838

The row \(\alpha = 0.9\) answers the President: the same maximum distance as the p-center (19), with a total distance almost equal to the p-median's (1465 against 1444). With \(\alpha = 1\) the model looks only at \(D_{\max}\) and, among the many solutions with \(D_{\max} = 19\), may pick one with a terrible total (1838). Note also the difference in scale: the total is in the thousands, \(D_{\max}\) in the tens, which is why \(\alpha\) must be high for \(D_{\max}\) to "weigh".

"Dear Board, now deciding which solution is best is up to you!" Testing different values of \(\alpha\) gives different compromises; the choice depends on the decision-maker's preferences. The search for compromise solutions is the field of multi-objective optimization.

Conclusions

  • The p-median and p-center models are the basic examples of location models, designed to optimize the location of facilities in a region.
  • \(p\) is a parameter: a sensitivity analysis on its value is often meaningful (how much does service worsen going from 6 to 5 branches?).
  • In general, location models support strategic or tactical decisions about the number, position and size of warehouses, stores, offices, emergency services and more.

Key takeaways

  • p-median: \(\min \sum_i \sum_j c_{ij} d_j x_{ij}\) with exactly \(p\) open facilities; minimizes the demand-weighted total (average) distance. NP-hard.
  • Two levels: once the open facilities \(S\) are chosen, each customer goes to the closest one; the hard part is \(S\).
  • Teitz and Bart: swap local search on \(S\), one candidate \(k\) at a time, labels \(\ell \in \{1, 0, -1\}\); after a successful swap the closed sites become to-try again.
  • p-center: \(\min D_{\max}\) with \(\sum_i c_{ij} x_{ij} \le D_{\max}\) for each customer; fairer, but blind to the total.
  • Compromise: \(\min \alpha D_{\max} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{total}\); as \(\alpha\) moves we travel along the Pareto front, and the choice belongs to the decision-maker.
