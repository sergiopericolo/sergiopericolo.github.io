Choice Under Uncertainty

en

Choice Under Uncertainty

Chapter 1 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). How rational agents assess and price risk.

Three tools:

  1. Expected utility and the risk premium — people rank gambles by expected utility, not expected money. The risk premium is what they pay to avoid risk (e.g. insurance).
  2. Risk aversion and asset demand — how much to put in risky assets.
  3. Portfolio choice under CARA — a utility function where the risky investment does not depend on wealth.

Risk vs. uncertainty

"Uncertainty is the very condition to impel men to exert themselves." — F. H. Knight

Risk (roulette) Uncertainty (F1 race)
Probabilities Known (1/37 or 1/38) Unknown, context-dependent
Outcome space Finite, well defined Vast, poorly defined
Repeatability Identical trials Each event unique
Information Complete rules Incomplete, ambiguous
Tools Probability theory Scenarios, judgment
Expert role Calculation Interpretation
  • Risk = known unknowns: measurable, insurable, diversifiable.
  • Uncertainty = unknown unknowns: no reliable distribution.
  • Knight (1921): risk is measurable; uncertainty is not, and it is the source of economic profit.
  • Keynes (1937): radical uncertainty; animal spirits and convention replace probabilities; liquidity preference is a response to it.

Key point: Knightian uncertainty cannot be modelled away — it needs different decision frameworks. This chapter deals with risk, where decision theory applies:

\[E[U] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \, U(x_i)\]

Risk management tools: probability and statistics, diversification, insurance, hedging.

Expected value and its limits

The EV criterion

Mike pays \(x\) to flip a coin: heads he gets 10, tails 0.

\[E[\text{game}] = 0.5(-x) + 0.5(10 - x) = 5 - x\]

EV is positive only if \(x \le 5\), so by EV Mike pays at most 5.

Expected value = long-run average of outcomes: \(E[\tilde X] = \sum_n p_n X_n\). For a fair die:

\[E[\tilde X] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = 3.5\]

Law of Large Numbers

The running average \(\bar X_n = S_n / n\) is noisy for small \(n\) and converges to \(E[\tilde X]\) as \(n \to \infty\). The slides' 10 rolls (4, 2, 5, 1, 6, 3, 4, 6, 2, 5) give \(\bar X_{10} = 38/10 = 3.80\).

Why people still play:

  • Dream factor: a small, sure cost for a life-changing prize.
  • Perception: people ignore EV and see that "someone always wins".
  • Biases: availability heuristic (we hear of winners), optimism bias, gambler's fallacy.
  • Entertainment: a ticket is bought like a movie ticket.
  • Social: heavy advertising, office and family pools.

The St. Petersburg paradox

Bernoulli's game: flip a fair coin until the first heads; if it comes on flip \(k\), win \(2^k\).

\[E[\text{game}] = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 4 + \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 8 + \dots = 1 + 1 + 1 + \dots = \infty\]

By EV one should pay any finite price to play; in reality people pay little.

Bernoulli's resolution: money is not valued linearly. Utility grows at a diminishing rate — the first dollar is worth more than the hundredth.

Diminishing marginal utility

Example: homeless Mike finds a ticket paying 1,000 or 0 with equal odds (EV = 500). Mr. Y offers 499 for sure.

  • EV criterion: reject, since 499 < 500.
  • Utility criterion: the first 499 means food and shelter; the next 501 add less; ending with 0 is catastrophic. Mike should accept.

Each extra unit consumed adds less satisfaction (illustrative values):

  • Diminishing marginal utility is why people maximise expected utility, not expected value.
  • A high-EV gamble can still be undesirable if it is very risky.

Expected utility and the risk premium

Expected utility

Weight each outcome by its utility:

\[E[U(\tilde x)] = \sum_i p_i \, U(x_i)\]

Mike accepts Mr. Y's offer if \(\tfrac12 U(1000) + \tfrac12 U(0) < U(499)\). With a concave \(U\) such that \(U(0)=0\), \(U(499)=10\), \(U(1000)=16\):

\[\text{LHS} = \tfrac12 (16) + \tfrac12 (0) = 8 \; < \; \text{RHS} = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{accept}\]

Expected-utility maximisers with diminishing marginal utility prefer sure outcomes and avoid gambles unless the payoff justifies the risk.

Fair lotteries and risk attitudes

A fair lottery has zero expected net outcome: its price equals its expected payoff.

Example: payoffs 0, 5, 50 with probabilities 0.5, 0.4, 0.1 give \(E = 0 + 2 + 5 = 7\). It is fair at a price of 7.

Facing a fair bet (e.g. pay 10 for an expected payoff of 10):

Attitude Utility Fair bet Pays to play
Risk-averse Concave, \(U'' < 0\) Declines Less than 10
Risk-neutral Linear, \(U'' = 0\) Indifferent Exactly 10
Risk-loving Convex, \(U'' > 0\) Accepts More than 10

Risk aversion: Mike with \(U(W) = W^{1/2}\)

Power (CRRA) utility \(U(W) = W^{1/2}\):

\[U'(W) = \tfrac12 W^{-1/2} > 0, \qquad U''(W) = -\tfrac14 W^{-3/2} < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{risk-averse}\]

Lottery: 20 or 0 with equal odds.

  • Expected wealth: \(E[\tilde W] = 10\).
  • Expected utility: \(E[U(\tilde W)] = \tfrac12 \sqrt{20} + \tfrac12 \sqrt{0} \approx 2.2\).
  • Utility of the sure 10: \(U(10) = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\).

Since \(E[U(\tilde W)] < U(E[\tilde W])\), Mike prefers the sure 10 and will pay to avoid the risk.

Certainty equivalent \(W_{CE}\): the sure wealth with the same utility as the lottery.

\[\sqrt{W_{CE}} = 2.2 \;\Rightarrow\; W_{CE} = 2.2^2 \approx 4.8\]

Risk premium \(\pi\): what Mike pays to avoid the lottery.

\[\pi = E[\tilde W] - W_{CE} = 10 - 4.8 = 5.2\]

\(\pi = 5.2\) is the maximum acceptable premium:

  • \(\pi = 4.5\): \(U(10 - 4.5) = \sqrt{5.5} \approx 2.3 > 2.2\) → paying is better than gambling.
  • \(\pi = 6\): \(U(10 - 6) = \sqrt{4} = 2 < 2.2\) → premium too high.

So any \(\pi \in [0, 5.2]\) is worth paying.

Risk-neutral, \(U(W) = W\): \(E[U(\tilde W)] = 10 = U(10)\). Indifferent; picks the highest EV; \(\pi = 0\).

Risk-loving, \(U(W) = W^2\) (\(U' = 2W > 0\), \(U'' = 2 > 0\)): \(E[U(\tilde W)] = \tfrac12 (400) + \tfrac12 (0) = 200 > U(10) = 100\), so the lottery wins.

\[W_{CE} = \sqrt{200} \approx 14.14, \qquad \pi = 10 - 14.14 = -4.14\]

A negative premium: the lottery is worth 14.14 to this person; they would pay to play rather than take the sure 10.

Switch utility and move \(\pi\). Left: curve, chord (expected utility) and \(W_{CE}\). Right: \(U(E[W]-\pi)\) against \(E[U]\); the shaded area is where paying \(\pi\) beats gambling.

In short: risk-averse agents demand compensation for risk (\(\pi > 0\)); risk lovers pay for it (\(\pi < 0\)).

Measuring the risk premium: Arrow–Pratt

The premium should grow with both the variability of outcomes and the agent's risk aversion:

\[\pi \approx \tfrac12 \, \lambda_W \, \sigma_W^2, \qquad \lambda_W = -\frac{U''(\bar W)}{U'(\bar W)}\]

\(\lambda_W\) is the Arrow–Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion.

Derivation. Let \(\bar W = E[\tilde W]\) and \(\sigma_W^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\tilde W)\).

  1. Second-order Taylor expansion of \(U(\tilde W)\) around \(\bar W\): \(U(\tilde W) \approx U(\bar W) + U'(\bar W)(\tilde W - \bar W) + \tfrac12 U''(\bar W)(\tilde W - \bar W)^2\).
  2. Take expectations (\(E[\tilde W - \bar W] = 0\)): \(E[U(\tilde W)] \approx U(\bar W) + \tfrac12 U''(\bar W)\sigma_W^2\).
  3. First-order expansion of the CE: \(U(W_{CE}) \approx U(\bar W) + U'(\bar W)(W_{CE} - \bar W)\).
  4. Set \(E[U(\tilde W)] = U(W_{CE})\): \(U'(\bar W)(W_{CE} - \bar W) = \tfrac12 U''(\bar W)\sigma_W^2\).
  5. Solve for \(\pi = \bar W - W_{CE}\): \(\pi = \tfrac12 \lambda_W \sigma_W^2\).

With \(\sigma_W^2 > 0\), \(U' > 0\), \(U'' < 0\), the premium is positive.

Risk aversion and asset demand

CARA utility

\[U(W) = \frac{1 - e^{-cW}}{c}, \qquad \lambda_W = -\frac{U''(W)}{U'(W)} = -\frac{-c\,e^{-cW}}{e^{-cW}} = c\]

Absolute risk aversion is constant and equal to \(c\).

If \(\tilde W \sim N(\mu_W, \sigma_W^2)\):

\[E[U(\tilde W)] = \frac{1 - e^{-c\mu_W + \frac{c^2 \sigma_W^2}{2}}}{c}\]

Maximising it is equivalent to maximising the mean–variance objective:

\[O = \mu_W - \tfrac12 c \, \sigma_W^2\]

One risky asset

Initial wealth 1, split between a risky asset \(\tilde r \sim N(\mu_r, \sigma_r^2)\) (weight \(w\)) and a riskless one with return \(r_f\):

\[W_1 = w(1 + \tilde r) + (1 - w)(1 + r_f) = 1 + r_f + w(\tilde r - r_f)\]

\[W_1 \sim N\big(1 + r_f + w(\mu_r - r_f), \; w^2 \sigma_r^2\big)\]

The investor maximises

\[O(w) = 1 + r_f + w(\mu_r - r_f) - \tfrac12 c \, w^2 \sigma_r^2\]

and the first-order condition gives

\[w^* = \frac{\mu_r - r_f}{c \, \sigma_r^2}\]

  • Higher risk aversion \(c\) → lower \(w^*\).
  • Higher excess return \(\mu_r - r_f\) → higher \(w^*\).
  • Higher variance \(\sigma_r^2\) → lower \(w^*\).
  • Initial wealth does not appear: under CARA the amount in risky assets does not depend on wealth.

N risky assets

\[\max_{\mathbf w} \; O = \mathbf w^\top(\boldsymbol\mu_r - r_f \mathbf 1) - \tfrac12 c \, \mathbf w^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma \mathbf w\]

\[\mathbf w^* = (c\boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_r - r_f \mathbf 1), \qquad w_f^* = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^*\]

The covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) brings in diversification; higher \(c\) shrinks exposure to every risky asset.

Key takeaways

  • Risk has known probabilities; uncertainty does not.
  • Expected value fails (St. Petersburg, Powerball, Mike's ticket) because it ignores risk.
  • Diminishing marginal utility → concave \(U\) → people maximise expected utility.
  • Certainty equivalent: \(U(W_{CE}) = E[U(\tilde W)]\). Risk premium: \(\pi = E[\tilde W] - W_{CE}\).
  • \(\pi > 0\) if risk-averse, \(= 0\) if risk-neutral, \(< 0\) if risk-loving.
  • Arrow–Pratt: \(\pi \approx \tfrac12 \lambda_W \sigma_W^2\) with \(\lambda_W = -U''/U'\).
  • CARA + normal returns → mean–variance objective → \(w^* = (\mu_r - r_f)/(c\sigma_r^2)\), independent of wealth.
// parse reads the shared flags, allowed anywhere.
func parse(c command, args []string) (*ctx, error) {
	cx := &ctx{}
	fs := flag.NewFlagSet(c.name, flag.ContinueOnError)
	fs.SetOutput(io.Discard)
	fs.StringVar(&cx.site, "site", "", "")
	fs.StringVar(&cx.lang, "lang", "", "")
	fs.StringVar(&cx.from, "from", "", "")
	fs.StringVar(&cx.like, "like", "", "")
	fs.StringVar(&cx.out, "out", "", "")
	fs.StringVar(&cx.addr, "addr", ":8080", "")
	fs.StringVar(&cx.msg, "m", "update", "")
	fs.BoolVar(&cx.build, "build", false, "")
	fs.BoolVar(&cx.del, "delete", false, "")
	fs.BoolVar(&cx.force, "force", false, "")
	for {
		if err := fs.Parse(args); errors.Is(err, flag.ErrHelp) {
			fmt.Printf("usage: %s\n%s\n\n%s", usage(c), c.help, flagsHelp)
			return nil, err
		} else if err != nil {
			return nil, usageError(err.Error())
		}
		if fs.NArg() == 0 {
			return cx, nil
		}
		cx.args, args = append(cx.args, fs.Arg(0)), fs.Args()[1:]
	}
}
