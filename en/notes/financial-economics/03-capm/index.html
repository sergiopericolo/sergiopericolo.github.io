The Capital Asset Pricing Model

en

The Capital Asset Pricing Model

Chapter 3 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). From one investor's optimal portfolio (Chapter 2) to market equilibrium: if everyone holds the tangency portfolio, it must be the market portfolio, and only systematic risk (beta) is priced.

Roadmap:

  1. The market portfolio — market clearing, why it is efficient and equal to \(T\).
  2. Deriving the CAPM — from the optimality condition to beta.
  3. The Security Market Line — SML vs. CML, the CAPM as a factor model.

The setting

MPT assumptions Equilibrium conditions
N risky assets with \((\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) Many price-taking investors, no market power
A risk-free asset \(r_f\) Fixed asset supply
Frictionless markets, infinite divisibility Markets clear: supply = demand
Symmetric information, mean–variance preferences Prices adjust to expected returns

All investors use the same information set, so they hold risky assets in the same proportions; their collective choices pin down prices.

The market portfolio

The market value of asset \(i\) is the value of all its traded shares. In equilibrium aggregate demand equals supply. With \(L\) investors of initial wealth \(W_0^l\) who put a fraction \(w_i^l\) in asset \(i\):

\[W^i = \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_i^l\,W_0^l\]

Summing over assets, total asset value = total wealth:

\[W^M = \sum_{i=1}^{N+1} W^i = \sum_{l=1}^{L} W_0^l\]

The market weights are wealth-weighted averages of individual weights:

\[w_i^M = \frac{W^i}{W^M} = \sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{W_0^l}{\sum_{l} W_0^l}\right) w_i^l, \qquad \mathbf w^M = \sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{W_0^l}{\sum_{l} W_0^l}\right)\mathbf w^l\]

So the market portfolio is a convex combination of investors' portfolios. Each of them lies on the CML, the CML is convex, hence the market portfolio is efficient.

The risk-free asset is in zero net supply: every lender faces a borrower.

\[W^{N+1} = \sum_{l} w_{N+1}^l W_0^l = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; w_{N+1}^M = 0\]

The market portfolio contains only risky assets and lies on the CML. The only such portfolio is the tangency one:

Proposition. The market portfolio coincides with the tangency portfolio, \(M = T\). It is efficient, and expected returns depend on covariance with \(M\) — on beta.

Deriving the CAPM

From Chapter 2, an efficient portfolio \(p\) with return \(\mu_p\) has weights

\[\mathbf w_p = \lambda\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f), \qquad \lambda = \frac{\mu_p - r_f}{(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)}\]

and the tangency portfolio is \(\mathbf w_T = \dfrac{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)}{\mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)}\).

Step by step.

  1. Rewrite the FOC: \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w_p = \lambda(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\). The left side is the vector of covariances of each asset with \(p\): \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w_p = \mathrm{cov}(\tilde{\mathbf r}, \tilde r_p)\).
  2. Premultiply by \(\mathbf w_p'\): \(\sigma_p^2 = \lambda(\mu_p - r_f)\), so \(1/\lambda = (\mu_p - r_f)/\sigma_p^2 \equiv \zeta\). Every efficient portfolio satisfies \[\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f = \zeta\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w_p, \qquad \zeta = \frac{\mu_p - r_f}{\sigma_p^2}\] Risk premia are proportional to covariances with \(p\).
  3. Define \(\boldsymbol\beta_p \equiv \boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w_p / \sigma_p^2\), i.e. \(\beta_{i,p} = \mathrm{cov}(\tilde r_i, \tilde r_p)/\sigma_p^2\): \[\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f = \boldsymbol\beta_p(\mu_p - r_f)\]
  4. Take \(p = T\) and use the equilibrium result \(T = M\):

\[\boxed{E[\tilde r_i] - r_f = \beta_{i,M}\big(E[\tilde r_M] - r_f\big), \qquad \beta_{i,M} = \frac{\mathrm{cov}(\tilde r_i, \tilde r_M)}{\sigma_M^2}}\]

\(\beta_{i,M}\) measures asset \(i\)'s contribution to the risk of \(M\). Only systematic risk is priced: idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away for free, so it earns no premium.

Roll's critique

The relation in step 3 holds for any efficient portfolio \(p\), not only for \(M\). Hence:

  • The CAPM is the statement that it holds for \(p = M\), the true market portfolio.
  • But \(M\) (all assets: stocks, bonds, real estate, human capital…) is unobservable; tests use proxies such as the S&P 500.
  • Every test is therefore a joint test of the CAPM and of the proxy's efficiency. If the proxy is efficient the relation holds mechanically; if not, a rejection may only mean the proxy is wrong.

The Security Market Line

\[E[\tilde r_i] = r_f + \beta_{i,M}\big(E[\tilde r_M] - r_f\big)\]

This straight line in \((\beta, E[r])\) space is the SML.

  • Intercept \(r_f\) (at \(\beta = 0\)), slope = market risk premium; the market sits at \(\beta = 1\).
  • High-\(\beta\) assets must offer higher expected returns; low-\(\beta\) assets lower.
  • A negative-\(\beta\) asset is a hedge: its expected return can be below \(r_f\).
  • An asset above the line has a positive alpha (it is cheap: too much return for its beta); below the line, negative alpha.

Move \(r_f\) and the market premium; the table shows each asset's CAPM return and its alpha.

CML vs. SML

Capital Market Line Security Market Line
Axes \((\sigma_p, E[r_p])\) \((\beta_p, E[r_p])\)
Risk measure Total risk \(\sigma\) Systematic risk \(\beta\)
Applies to Efficient portfolios only Any asset or portfolio
Slope Sharpe ratio of \(M\): \((E[r_M] - r_f)/\sigma_M\) Market premium \(E[r_M] - r_f\)

An inefficient asset lies below the CML (it carries unpriced idiosyncratic risk) but, if the CAPM holds, on the SML.

The CAPM as a linear factor model

General form: \(E[\tilde r_i] = \alpha + \boldsymbol\beta'\mathbf f\). The CAPM restricts it to one factor, \(\mathbf f = \tilde r_M\), with \(\alpha = r_f\).

  • Multi-factor extensions, e.g. Fama–French: \(\mathbf f = [\tilde r_M, SMB, HML]'\) (Chapter 6).
  • Conditional CAPM: \(\beta\) changes with the state of the economy.
  • Empirical challenges: the market proxy, time-varying betas, anomalies such as momentum (Chapter 4).

What equilibrium requires

For \(T = M\) we need homogeneous expectations (everyone agrees on \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\)), perfect markets (no taxes, costs or short-sale limits) and mean–variance investors. Then

\[\mathbf w^M = \frac{\text{market capitalisation of each asset}}{\text{total market wealth}}\]

and markets clear asset by asset.

Key takeaways

  • Market clearing: total asset value = total wealth; \(\mathbf w^M\) is a wealth-weighted average of investors' portfolios.
  • The risk-free asset is in zero net supply → \(M\) holds only risky assets → \(M = T\), efficient.
  • Efficient portfolios satisfy \(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f = \zeta\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w_p\): premia ∝ covariances.
  • CAPM: \(E[\tilde r_i] - r_f = \beta_{i,M}(E[\tilde r_M] - r_f)\), \(\beta_{i,M} = \mathrm{cov}(\tilde r_i, \tilde r_M)/\sigma_M^2\).
  • Only systematic risk is priced; negative \(\beta\) → expected return below \(r_f\).
  • SML (β, any asset) vs. CML (σ, efficient portfolios only).
  • Roll: \(M\) is unobservable, every test is joint with the proxy.
