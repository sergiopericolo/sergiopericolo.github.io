The Consumption CAPM

en

The Consumption CAPM

Chapter 5 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). The CAPM is static: one period, mean and variance. The Consumption CAPM is an intertemporal general equilibrium model where assets are priced by how they move with consumption, and it runs into two famous puzzles.

Roadmap:

  1. The model — Lucas tree, Euler equation, stochastic discount factor.
  2. Deriving the CCAPM — risk premia as covariances with marginal utility.
  3. Testing it — CRRA and log-normal consumption, U.S. data 1930–2023.
  4. The equity premium and risk-free rate puzzles — and the main proposed solutions.

From CAPM to CCAPM

Static models ignore that investors revise portfolios repeatedly as information arrives. The CCAPM (Rubinstein 1976; Breeden and Litzenberger 1978; Breeden 1979; Lucas 1978 with Euler equations) has investors decide recursively over many periods.

CAPM CCAPM
Horizon One period Many periods, recursive
Investor cares about Mean and variance of returns Expected utility of lifetime consumption
Risk is Covariance with the market Covariance with marginal utility of consumption
Focus Market risk Consumption smoothing

Representative agent: homogeneous preferences and endowments. It gives a tractable link between prices and aggregate risk, but ignores heterogeneity (different risk aversions) and cannot explain the pricing of idiosyncratic risks.

The tree model

The agent chooses the shares \(q_{t+1}\) of a tree (an asset paying dividend \(d_t\)) to solve

\[\max_{\{q_{t+1}\}} E_t\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta^k u(\tilde c_{t+k})\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad c_t + p_tq_{t+1} = d_tq_t + p_tq_t\]

with \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) the discount factor and \(u\) a VNM utility.

First-order condition

\[-p_t u'(c_t) + \beta E_t\big[u'(\tilde c_{t+1})(\tilde d_{t+1} + \tilde p_{t+1})\big] = 0\]

Marginal cost (today) = Marginal benefit (tomorrow)
Buying one more share costs \(p_t\) of consumption: \(p_tu'(c_t)\) It pays \(\tilde d_{t+1} + \tilde p_{t+1}\), valued at discounted marginal utility: \(\beta E_t[u'(\tilde c_{t+1})(\tilde d_{t+1} + \tilde p_{t+1})]\)

The agent invests until the utility lost today equals the discounted expected utility gained tomorrow.

Equilibrium with one tree: \(q_{t+1} = 1\) and \(c_t = d_t\), so \(u'(d_t)p_t = \beta E_t[u'(\tilde d_{t+1})(\tilde d_{t+1} + \tilde p_{t+1})]\). With many securities, the fundamental asset pricing equation:

\[p_{i,t} = E_t\Big[\beta\frac{u'(\tilde c_{t+1})}{u'(c_t)}\big(\tilde d_{i,t+1} + \tilde p_{i,t+1}\big)\Big]\]

The stochastic discount factor

Define the SDF (pricing kernel, intertemporal marginal rate of substitution):

\[\tilde m_{t+1} \equiv \beta\frac{u'(\tilde c_{t+1})}{u'(c_t)}, \qquad p_{i,t} = E_t\big[\tilde m_{t+1}(\tilde d_{i,t+1} + \tilde p_{i,t+1})\big]\]

Substitute \(\tilde p_{i,t+1}\) forward \(K\) times:

\[p_{i,t} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}E_t\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\tilde m_{t+j}\,\tilde d_{i,t+k}\Big] + E_t\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{K}\tilde m_{t+j}\,\tilde p_{i,t+K}\Big]\]

With no bubbles (transversality: the last term → 0 as \(K \to \infty\)):

\[p_{i,t} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta^k E_t\Big[\frac{u'(\tilde c_{t+k})}{u'(c_t)}\tilde d_{i,t+k}\Big]\]

Risk-neutral special case: \(u(c) = c \Rightarrow \tilde m_{t+1} = \beta\). If a riskless rate exists, \(\beta = 1/(1 + r_f)\) and the price is the classic discounted dividend sum \(p_{i,t} = \sum_k E_t[\tilde d_{i,t+k}]/(1 + r_f)^k\).

Key points: \(\tilde m\) is the same for all assets; prices depend on how payoffs co-move with \(\tilde m\). The CAPM, for tractability, replaces the SDF with the market return.

The riskless security

A discount bond pays 1 unit of consumption at \(t+1\):

\[p_{f,t} = E_t\Big[\beta\frac{u'(\tilde c_{t+1})}{u'(c_t)}\Big], \qquad \frac{1}{1 + r_{f,t+1}} = E_t[\tilde m_{t+1}]\]

In the Lucas tree the riskless asset is in zero net supply: the representative investor holds zero units in equilibrium, and the price is such that there is no excess demand.

Deriving the CCAPM

With the gross return \(1 + \tilde r_{i,t+1} = (\tilde d_{i,t+1} + \tilde p_{i,t+1})/p_{i,t}\), the pricing equation becomes the Euler equation

\[1 = E_t\big[\tilde m_{t+1}(1 + \tilde r_{i,t+1})\big]\]

Use \(E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] + \mathrm{cov}(X, Y)\):

\[1 = E_t[\tilde m_{t+1}]\,E_t[1 + \tilde r_{i,t+1}] + \mathrm{cov}_t(\tilde m_{t+1}, \tilde r_{i,t+1})\]

Divide by \(E_t[\tilde m_{t+1}] = 1/(1 + r_{f,t+1})\) and rearrange:

\[\boxed{E_t[\tilde r_{i,t+1}] - r_{f,t+1} = -(1 + r_{f,t+1})\,\mathrm{cov}_t\Big(\beta\frac{u'(\tilde c_{t+1})}{u'(c_t)}, \tilde r_{i,t+1}\Big)}\]

  • Negative covariance with marginal utility: the asset pays well when consumption is high (MU low), badly when you need it most → it must offer a higher return.
  • Positive covariance: it pays when MU is high (bad times) → it is insurance, a lower return is accepted.

Log utility: \(u = \log c \Rightarrow \tilde m_{t+1} = \beta c_t/\tilde c_{t+1}\).

Link to the CAPM. Take an asset whose return is perfectly correlated with the SDF, \(\tilde R_{c,t+1} = \tilde m_{t+1}\). Then

\[E_t[\tilde r_{i,t+1}] - r_{f,t+1} = \beta_{i,c}\big(E_t[\tilde r_{c,t+1}] - r_{f,t+1}\big), \qquad \beta_{i,c} = \frac{\mathrm{cov}_t(\tilde m_{t+1}, \tilde r_{i,t+1})}{\mathrm{Var}_t(\tilde m_{t+1})}\]

The same beta form, with the IMRS in place of the market portfolio. Common insight: higher expected returns compensate exposure to aggregate risk; the two models only measure it differently.

Testing the CCAPM

Can the CCAPM explain the observed equity risk premium? Assume:

  • CRRA utility \(U(c) = \dfrac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), \(u'(c) = c^{-\gamma}\), risk aversion \(\gamma\);
  • consumption growth and dividend growth i.i.d. log-normal, jointly normal (in the book's derivation the equity claim pays consumption).

Risk-free rate: \(\dfrac{1}{1 + r_f} = E\big[\beta(c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}\big]\), which gives

\[\ln r_f = -\ln\beta + \gamma\mu_{\Delta c} - \tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_{\Delta c}^2\]

  • \(\gamma\mu_{\Delta c}\): with growing consumption, people want to borrow against the future → high rates.
  • \(-\tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma^2_{\Delta c}\): precautionary saving pushes rates down.

Equity: the price–dividend ratio is a geometric sum,

\[\frac{p_t}{d_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta^j e^{j[(1-\gamma)\mu_{\Delta c} + \frac12(1-\gamma)^2\sigma_{\Delta c}^2]} = \frac{\beta e^{(1-\gamma)\mu_{\Delta c} + \frac12(1-\gamma)^2\sigma_{\Delta c}^2}}{1 - \beta e^{(1-\gamma)\mu_{\Delta c} + \frac12(1-\gamma)^2\sigma_{\Delta c}^2}}\]

and the expected return is

\[\ln E(r_i) = -\ln\beta + \gamma\mu_{\Delta c} + \tfrac12\sigma_{\Delta c}^2(2\gamma - \gamma^2)\]

Subtracting, the equity risk premium:

\[\boxed{\ln E(r_i) - \ln r_f = \gamma\,\sigma_{\Delta c}^2}\]

It grows with risk aversion and with consumption volatility. But U.S. consumption is smooth.

The data: U.S. 1930–2023

Panel Quantity Value
A. Consumption growth Mean \(E[\Delta c]\) 2.03%
Variance \(\sigma^2_{\Delta c}\) 0.086%
Std. dev. \(\sigma_{\Delta c}\) 2.94%
B. Asset returns Mean equity return \(E[r_M]\) 8.59%
Mean risk-free return \(E[r_f]\) 0.11%
Equity premium (stocks − 1m T-bill) 8.48%
C. Alternatives ERP (stocks − 3m T-bill) 8.19%
ERP (stocks − 10y T-bond) 6.62%

Sources: BEA, FRED, BLS, Fama–French, Damodaran.

The model with \(\gamma = 10\), \(\beta = 0.99\)

\[\ln r_f = 0.01005 + 10(0.0203) - \tfrac12(100)(0.00086) = 17.01\%\]

\[\ln E(r_i) = 0.01005 + 0.203 + \tfrac12(20 - 100)(0.00086) = 17.87\%\]

\[\text{ERP} = 10 \times 0.086\% = 0.86\%\]

Model (\(\gamma = 10\)) Data Gap
Risk-free rate 17.01% 0.11% overshoots by > 100×
Equity premium 0.86% 8.48% undershoots by ≈ 10×

Equity premium puzzle (Mehra and Prescott): why do stocks earn so much more than bills when consumption — the thing that matters for utility — is so smooth?

Move \(\gamma\). The premium only reaches the data near \(\gamma \approx 100\) (right panel). Watch the risk-free rate on the left: it first climbs above 20% (the \(\gamma\mu\) term) and then, for large \(\gamma\), the precautionary term \(-\tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma^2\) takes over and drags it far below zero. Either way it is nowhere near the 0.11% in the data. Lower \(\sigma\) (smoother consumption) and the required \(\gamma\) rises further.

What \(\gamma\) matches the data?

\[8.482\% = \gamma \cdot 0.086\% \;\Rightarrow\; \gamma = 98.63\]

The literature considers \(3 \le \gamma \le 10\) plausible. With such a high \(\gamma\) the model cannot deliver a sensible risk-free rate: the risk-free rate puzzle (Weil, 1989). The slides phrase it as an "unrealistically high" \(r_f\), which is what happens for the plausible range \(\gamma \le 10\) (above 15%).

Note: with the log-normal formula above and \(\sigma_{\Delta c} = 2.94\%\), at \(\gamma \approx 99\) the term \(-\tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma^2_{\Delta c} \approx -4.2\) dominates and \(\ln r_f\) turns hugely negative rather than high. The point is the same — no single \(\gamma\) matches both the premium and \(r_f\) — but it is worth knowing if the exam asks for the number.

The puzzle over time

The ERP is not constant; studies document a decline from the 1960s to the 1990s (Siegel 1992; Jagannathan et al. 2001).

Pre-1975 Post-1975
Equity premium 11.96% 7.01%
Consumption volatility \(\sigma_{\Delta c}\) 2.92% 1.72%
Implied \(\gamma = \text{ERP}/\sigma^2_{\Delta c}\) 11.96 / 0.085 = 140.7 7.01 / 0.0296 = 236.8

A lower premium should be easier to match, but consumption volatility fell even more (better risk-sharing and smoothing, less uncertainty). Lower volatility → higher required \(\gamma\): the puzzle gets worse. With post-1975 averages, weaker precautionary saving pushes the implied \(r_f\) to about 21.8%.

Conclusion: the standard CCAPM cannot reconcile observed equity premia and risk-free rates.

Alternative explanations

Model Idea Why it raises the ERP
Habit formation (Campbell and Cochrane, 1999) Utility depends on consumption relative to a habit level Risk aversion rises when consumption nears the habit: counter-cyclical, time-varying risk aversion
Rare disasters (Barro, 2006, 2009) Small probability of catastrophic shocks Tail risk earns a large premium with reasonable \(\gamma\); explains skewness too
Long-run risk (Bansal and Yaron, 2004) Persistent, low-frequency shocks to growth; Epstein–Zin utility separates risk aversion from the EIS Investors fear slow-moving growth risks and demand compensation

Key takeaways

  • The CCAPM is intertemporal: assets are priced by the SDF \(\tilde m_{t+1} = \beta u'(\tilde c_{t+1})/u'(c_t)\), with \(p_t = E_t[\tilde m_{t+1}x_{t+1}]\) and \(1 + r_f = 1/E_t[\tilde m_{t+1}]\).
  • Risk premium: \(E_t[\tilde r_i] - r_f = -(1 + r_f)\mathrm{cov}_t(\tilde m_{t+1}, \tilde r_i)\): assets that pay in good times earn more.
  • CRRA + log-normal: \(\ln r_f = -\ln\beta + \gamma\mu - \tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma^2\), ERP \(= \gamma\sigma^2\).
  • U.S. data: ERP ≈ 8.5%, \(r_f\) ≈ 0.1%, \(\sigma_{\Delta c}\) ≈ 2.9% → \(\gamma \approx 99\) needed (equity premium puzzle) and then \(r_f\) is far too high (risk-free rate puzzle).
  • Post-1975 the puzzle worsens (\(\gamma \approx 237\)) because consumption became smoother.
  • Proposed fixes: habit formation, rare disasters, long-run risk.
