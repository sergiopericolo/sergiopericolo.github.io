Arbitrage Pricing Theory and Multi-factor Models

Chapter 6 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). When the CAPM's single market factor fails to explain returns, two routes open: the APT (Ross, 1976), which prices assets by no-arbitrage with several factors, and empirical multi-factor models such as Fama–French.

Roadmap:

  1. Anomalies — what the CAPM cannot explain.
  2. The APT — factor model, well-diversified portfolios, arbitrage, \(\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf B\boldsymbol\lambda\); the CAPM as a special case.
  3. Multifactor analysis — the Fama–French three-factor model and its construction.
  4. Empirical applications — FF3 on five DJIA stocks; uncertainty factors (VIX, EPU).

Anomalies in asset returns

Early tests supported the CAPM (Black, Jensen and Scholes 1972; Fama and MacBeth 1973), in both time series and cross-section. From the late 1970s, anomalies:

Study Finding
Basu (1977) Low P/E firms earn higher returns
Banz (1981) Small caps beat the CAPM (size effect)
Stattman (1980), Rosenberg et al. (1985) Book-to-market matters (value effect)
Fama and French (1992, 1993) Small and high-B/M firms earn more → three-factor model

Fama–French evidence. Monthly NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ returns 1926–1991; 10 value-weighted portfolios rebalanced yearly, sorted on size or B/M; regress \(z_{p,t} = \alpha_p + \beta_{p,M}z_{M,t} + \varepsilon_{p,t}\) and test \(H_0: \alpha_p = 0\). Result: small-cap portfolios have significantly positive alphas, large caps significantly negative ones. One factor is not enough.

New factors: SMB (small minus big, the size premium) and HML (high minus low B/M, value beats growth). Later Fama–French added profitability and investment.

The APT

The CAPM has one source of systematic risk; the APT allows many (macroeconomic, industry, firm-specific), relies on no-arbitrage rather than equilibrium, and applies to well-diversified portfolios.

Factor model

\[z_i = \alpha_i + \boldsymbol\beta_i'\mathbf f + \varepsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, N \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mathbf z = \boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf B\mathbf f + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\]

  • \(\mathbf f\): \(\kappa\) common factors; \(\boldsymbol\beta_i\): factor loadings.
  • \(\varepsilon_i\): idiosyncratic, mean zero, uncorrelated with the factors.

A well-diversified portfolio has \(\varepsilon_p \approx 0\), so \(z_p = \alpha_p + \boldsymbol\beta_p'\mathbf f\) and, without arbitrage, \(\mu_p = \boldsymbol\beta_p'\boldsymbol\mu_f\): expected return is set only by exposures to common factors.

Arbitrage

An arbitrage portfolio \(\mathbf w\) is

  • costless: \(\mathbf w'\mathbf 1 = 0\);
  • riskless: \(\mathbf w'\mathbf B = \mathbf 0\) (no factor exposure);
  • profitable: \(\mathbf w'\boldsymbol\mu > 0\).

In equilibrium a costless riskless portfolio must earn zero.

Proposition (APT). With no arbitrage and no idiosyncratic risk, expected excess returns are linear in the loadings: \[\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf B\boldsymbol\lambda\] \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) is the vector of factor risk premia; when the factors are themselves excess returns, \(\boldsymbol\lambda = \boldsymbol\mu_f\).

Proof idea (projection).

  1. Project \(\boldsymbol\mu\) on the loadings: \(\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf B\boldsymbol\lambda + \boldsymbol\nu\), with \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) minimising \(\boldsymbol\nu'\boldsymbol\nu\), so \(\boldsymbol\nu'\mathbf B = \mathbf 0\) (residual orthogonal to the factor space).
  2. Use the residual as a portfolio, \(\mathbf w = \boldsymbol\nu\): it is riskless, \(\mathrm{Var} = \boldsymbol\nu'\mathbf B\boldsymbol\Sigma_f\mathbf B'\boldsymbol\nu = 0\), and earns \(\boldsymbol\nu'\boldsymbol\mu = \boldsymbol\nu'\boldsymbol\nu \ge 0\) (costless after adding a constant factor).
  3. If \(\boldsymbol\nu \ne \mathbf 0\) that is a riskless, costless profit — a contradiction. So \(\boldsymbol\nu = \mathbf 0\) and \(\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf B\boldsymbol\lambda\).

Geometrically: \(\boldsymbol\mu\) must lie in \(\mathrm{span}(\mathbf B)\). The result is exact as \(N \to \infty\) (Ross, 1976) and approximate for finite \(N\).

The CAPM as a special case

One factor, the market: \(\tilde r_i = E(\tilde r_i) + \beta_i[\tilde r_M - E(\tilde r_M)]\) (with \(\varepsilon_i = 0\)).

  1. Mix assets \(i\) and \(j\): \(\tilde r_P = \alpha\tilde r_i + (1-\alpha)\tilde r_j\) with \(\alpha\beta_i + (1-\alpha)\beta_j = 0\). \(P\) has no risk.
  2. No arbitrage: a riskless portfolio earns \(r_f\), so \(\alpha E(\tilde r_i) + (1-\alpha)E(\tilde r_j) = r_f\).
  3. Rearranging, the price of risk is the same for both assets: \[\xi = \frac{E(\tilde r_i) - r_f}{\beta_i} = \frac{E(\tilde r_j) - r_f}{\beta_j}\]
  4. The market has \(\beta = 1\), so \(\xi = E(\tilde r_M) - r_f\), and \(E(\tilde r_i) = r_f + \beta_i[E(\tilde r_M) - r_f]\): the CAPM.

Equivalently (Box 6.4): two portfolios \(p\), \(q\) with alphas; weights \(w_p = \beta_q/(\beta_q - \beta_p)\), \(w_q = -\beta_p/(\beta_q - \beta_p)\) give \(\beta_a = 0\), hence \(\alpha_a\) must be 0: any zero-beta portfolio has zero excess return.

Try it: here A sits on a line with price of risk 5%, B on one with 6%. Mix them to kill the beta and the mix does not earn \(r_f\): free money. Set \(E[r_B] = 10.5\%\) (\(= 3\% + 1.5 \times 5\%\)) and the arbitrage disappears.

CAPM vs. APT

CAPM APT
Risk factors One: the market portfolio Several: macro, industry, firm-specific
Basis Equilibrium No-arbitrage
Assumptions Strong (mean–variance, homogeneous expectations) Weaker: just no arbitrage
Applies to All assets Mainly well-diversified portfolios
Pricing \(\mu_i = r_f + \beta_i(\mu_M - r_f)\) \(\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf B\boldsymbol\lambda\)

The APT is agnostic: it does not say which factors. Chen, Roll and Ross (1986) used macroeconomic factors; Fama and French (1993, 1996) used size and value.

The Fama–French three-factor model

\[z_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}z_{M,t} + \beta_{i,SMB}SMB_t + \beta_{i,HML}HML_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}\]

  • \(z_M\): market excess return.
  • SMB: return of small minus big stocks.
  • HML: return of high minus low book-to-market stocks (value minus growth).

Building the factors

Each June, with NYSE breakpoints:

  1. Split stocks by market cap at the median: Small (S) / Big (B).
  2. Independently split by B/M at the 30th/70th percentiles: Low (L) / Medium (M) / High (H).
  3. Intersect: six value-weighted portfolios S/L, S/M, S/H, B/L, B/M, B/H.
  4. Long–short factors:

\[HML_t = \tfrac12(r_{S/H} + r_{B/H}) - \tfrac12(r_{S/L} + r_{B/L}), \qquad SMB_t = \tfrac12(r_{S/H} + r_{S/L}) - \tfrac12(r_{B/H} + r_{B/L})\]

Low B/M Medium High B/M
Small S/L S/M S/H
Big B/L B/M B/H

HML is long the right column, short the left (the M portfolios are not used); averaging across size makes it roughly size-neutral. SMB is long the top row, short the bottom.

Original findings: SMB and HML loadings significant; higher loadings → higher expected returns; adjusted \(R^2 > 0.70\) for portfolios, robust across periods.

Uncertainty-augmented CAPM: VIX and EPU

The CAPM and FF3 omit momentum, liquidity and macro uncertainty. Do two "fear indices" help?

\[z_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}z_{M,t} + \beta_{i,VIX}\Delta VIX_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \qquad z_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}z_{M,t} + \beta_{i,EPU}\Delta EPU_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}\]

\(\Delta VIX\): change in CBOE implied volatility; \(\Delta EPU\): change in the U.S. Economic Policy Uncertainty index (Baker, Bloom and Davis, 2016).

Key takeaways

  • Anomalies (size, value, P/E) → the market factor alone is insufficient.
  • APT: \(\mathbf z = \boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf B\mathbf f + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\); no arbitrage on well-diversified portfolios ⇒ \(\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf B\boldsymbol\lambda\).
  • Arbitrage portfolio: costless (\(\mathbf w'\mathbf 1 = 0\)), riskless (\(\mathbf w'\mathbf B = 0\)), profitable — must not exist.
  • The CAPM is the one-factor APT: every zero-beta portfolio earns \(r_f\), so \((E[r_i] - r_f)/\beta_i\) is constant.
  • FF3: market + SMB + HML; factors built from 2×3 size–B/M sorts.
  • DJIA 2000–22: FF3 kills the alphas, adds some \(R^2\) (Merck, AmEx), not everywhere; VIX helps a little, EPU hardly.
