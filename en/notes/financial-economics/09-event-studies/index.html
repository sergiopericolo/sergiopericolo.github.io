Event Study Analysis

Chapter 9 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). How do prices react to news? Event studies isolate the effect of an event by comparing actual returns with what a pricing model (the CAPM) would have predicted, in a short window around the event.

Roadmap:

  1. Human behaviour vs. events — why reactions, not events, move prices.
  2. Methodologies — abnormal returns and CARs; the regression-based approach.
  3. Application — aviation disasters and U.S. transportation stocks: CARs, regressions, a trading strategy.

Human behaviour vs. events

"What is important in market fluctuations are not the events themselves but the human reactions to those events."

  • Prices aggregate interpretations of news, not the news itself.
  • Reactions can be biased: underreaction (beliefs updated too slowly) or overreaction (extrapolating too much from similar outcomes).
  • Emotions and herding amplify small shocks; prices are more volatile than fundamentals (Shiller, 1981); limits to arbitrage let mispricing persist.
  • Perception often matters more than reality; sensationalist media can heighten fear or excitement.

The event study method

Premise: in efficient markets new information is incorporated into prices quickly, so the price path in a short window around an event reveals its economic effect. Works for equities, bonds, options and derivatives, and tests informational efficiency.

History: Dolley (1933) studied stock splits 1921–1931 (prices rose in 57 of 95 cases). Today: firm events (earnings, M&A), economy-wide events (policy changes), non-economic events (wars, pandemics, disasters).

The sentiment channel

Investor sentiment (Baker and Wurgler, 2006): "a belief regarding future cash flows and investment risks that is not substantiated by available facts".

  • Sports: losses in soccer, cricket, rugby are followed by market declines (Edmans et al., 2007; Kaplansky and Levy, 2010a).
  • Aviation disasters: public anxiety → sell-offs of risky assets (Kaplansky and Levy, 2010b).
  • War: the 1973 Yom Kippur war's negative effect outweighed holiday effects (Kaplansky and Levy, 2012).
  • Pharma and epidemics (Donadelli et al., 2017): WHO alerts and media coverage of SARS, H1N1, Ebola raise pharmaceutical returns, more for small firms (agility, growth potential).
  • Climate transition risk (Barnett, 2024): a higher probability of climate policy significantly lowers returns of high-emission firms (coal, oil, mining, steel).

Methodology 1: abnormal returns and CARs

Expected returns come from the CAPM estimated on pre-event data:

\[z_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}z_{M,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat z_{i,t,e} = \hat\alpha_{i,e} + \hat\beta_{i,M,e}\,z_{M,t,e}\]

Abnormal return: actual minus expected,

\[AR_{i,t,e} = z_{i,t} - \hat z_{i,t,e}\]

Cumulative abnormal return over the event window:

\[CAR_{i,e} = \sum_{\tau = t-j}^{t+j} AR_{i,\tau,e}\]

Positive CAR = favourable reaction; negative = unfavourable; zero = no impact. CARs are then averaged across stocks and events (or across events for a portfolio), which raises statistical power.

Three windows

Window Days (example) Purpose
Estimation \(t-K\) … \(t-k\) (e.g. −250 … −30) Estimate \(\hat\alpha\), \(\hat\beta\) on clean, pre-event data
Event \(t-j\) … \(t+j\) (e.g. −1 … +5) Compute ARs and CARs
Post-event after \(t+j\) Check efficiency: no ARs should remain

The gap between estimation and event windows keeps the event's own impact out of the benchmark.

Steps: (1) define the event window; (2) choose the estimation period; (3) compute expected returns (CAPM or factor model); (4) ARs; (5) CARs; (6) test significance. Watch the estimation window length, the benchmark model, overlapping events.

What CARs tell you: the magnitude of the reaction, its speed, its persistence (temporary or permanent), and cross-sectional differences across firms or industries.

Simulate it. A shock on day +1 that fully reverses on day +2 leaves CAR ≈ 0 at the end — an overreaction. With one event the noise swamps everything; averaging over many events shrinks the band by \(1/\sqrt N\).

Methodology 2: regression-based event study

Kaplansky and Levy (2010b) estimate the event effect in a regression on the index return \(r_t\) that controls for confounding factors:

\[r_t = \delta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{3}\delta_{1,i}r_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^{4}\delta_{2,i}D_{i,t} + \delta_3T_t + \delta_4E_t + \varepsilon_t\]

Term What it controls Why
\(\sum\delta_{1,i}r_{t-i}\) Three lagged returns Index returns are autocorrelated
\(\sum\delta_{2,i}D_{i,t}\) Day-of-week dummies (Mon–Thu) Monday / weekend effect (Cho et al., 2007)
\(T_t\) First five days of the tax year Turn-of-year effect (Dyl and Maberly, 1992)
\(E_t\) Event dummy (1 on the event day) \(\delta_4\) is the parameter of interest

Same design: World Cup games (Kaplansky and Levy, 2010a), Yom Kippur (2012), pandemic news (Donadelli et al., 2017).

Extended attention. Disasters keep attention for days and can trigger herding. To capture delayed reactions and reversals, use three event dummies:

\[r_t = \delta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{3}\delta_{1,i}r_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^{4}\delta_{2,i}D_{i,t} + \delta_3T_t + \sum_{i=1}^{3}\delta_{4,i}E_{i,t} + \varepsilon_t\]

Coefficient Timing Meaning
\(\delta_{4,1}\) Event day Immediate reaction
\(\delta_{4,2}\) Day +1 Persistence or first reversal
\(\delta_{4,3}\) Day +2 Delayed effects, adjustment

Significance patterns speak to efficiency, signs to the direction of sentiment, magnitudes to the economic impact.

CARs

One-factor CAPM with the Fama–French market factor, estimation window \(t-250\) … \(t-30\), event window \(t-1\) … \(t+5\):

\[CAR_{it} = \sum_{\tau = t-1}^{t+5}\big(R_{i\tau} - \hat R_{i\tau}\big)\]

  • World: slight increase on days +1, +2, then a marked decline by day +3, negative through day +5; final CAR −0.24%. A lagged reaction: news of non-U.S. disasters diffuses more slowly (later media coverage).
  • Americas: immediate response — a significant drop on day +1, partial recovery by day +4.
  • Consistent with Kaplansky and Levy (2010b): sharp initial decline, reversal from day +3 (170 events, 1950–2007, U.S./European airlines).

  • Strong autocorrelation, a negative Monday effect, a positive turn-of-year effect.
  • World: negative event-day effect, rebound on days +1/+2 — none significant.
  • Americas: a significant drop of 0.68% on the event day and an almost exact rebound of 0.67% the next day: panic selling followed by rapid correction. Proximity intensifies the reaction.

Interpretation. Fear-driven sentiment moves prices: an initial overreaction, amplified by regional proximity (limited attention, availability bias). The mispricing is short-lived (corrected within two trading days) — exploitable by contrarian, sophisticated or international investors.

The aviation disaster effect (ADE) strategy

A zero-cost, event-driven strategy (short one asset to fund the other) that trades on the predictable post-disaster dip:

  • World (54 events, 10,570 days): ADE 231.90% cumulative vs. 222.24% buy-and-hold: +9.66%, consistently ahead after 1995.
  • Americas (16 events): stronger and significant coefficients → better signal quality and larger gains, especially in recent decades.

Predictable patterns exist; sentiment drives short-term moves; geographic proximity raises profitability.

Key takeaways

  • Events matter through reactions: under/overreaction, sentiment, herding, limits to arbitrage.
  • \(AR = z - (\hat\alpha + \hat\beta z_M)\) with parameters from a pre-event estimation window; \(CAR\) = sum of ARs over the event window; averaging across events adds power.
  • Regression approach: event dummies plus controls for autocorrelation, day-of-week and turn-of-year effects; several dummies capture reversals.
  • Aviation disasters: World CAR −0.24% with a lag; Americas a significant −0.68% day-one drop and +0.67% rebound → short-lived overreaction, a profitable ADE strategy (+9.66% over buy-and-hold globally).
