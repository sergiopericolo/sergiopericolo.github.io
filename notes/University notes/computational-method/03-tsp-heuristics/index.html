The Traveling Salesperson Problem: constructive heuristics and 2-opt

Lecture 3 of Computational Methods (Carlo Filippi, University of Brescia). A van must visit a set of customers and return to the depot along the shortest route. The Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) is much harder than the knapsack; here we see the two most used families of heuristics: constructive methods and local search.

Roadmap:

  1. The Wash Bowl case — a laundry, a truck, one tour a day.
  2. The TSP — graphs, Hamiltonian tours, symmetric and asymmetric versions, performance ratio and the triangle inequality.
  3. Constructive methods — nearest neighbor, three insertion methods, Clarke & Wright.
  4. Local search — k-opt moves, 2-opt and 3-opt.

The Wash Bowl case

Wash Bowl is a laundry in Brescia that, besides private customers, serves hotels in the city and the surrounding area. Every day a truck visits a group of hotels to deliver clean linen and pick up dirty linen; each hotel is visited twice a week on fixed days (e.g. Monday and Thursday). The truck has enough capacity for all the hotels of the day.

Costs are of two kinds:

  • loading/unloading: the driver's time at the hotels. It drops if each hotel knows in advance the (approximate) arrival time of the truck;
  • travel: driver time, fixed truck costs, fuel, vehicle wear. It drops if the route duration is minimized.

If the route is optimized and fixed in advance, arrival times can be communicated to the hotels: the two goals help each other. Every day's problem is: find a route starting and ending at the depot that visits all the hotels of the day and minimizes total travel time. It is a TSP.

The Traveling Salesperson Problem

Given \(n + 1\) points \(0, 1, \dots, n\), find a closed tour of minimum cost that visits every point exactly once. Usually the tour is driven by a vehicle, point 0 is the depot and \(1, \dots, n\) are the customers.

Formally we use a complete directed graph \(G = (V, A, c)\):

  • \(V = \{0, 1, \dots, n\}\) is the node set (0 is the depot);
  • \(A = \{(i, j) : i, j \in V,\ i \ne j\}\) is the arc set;
  • \(c = \{c_{ij}\}\) are the arc costs.

A Hamiltonian tour is a closed tour visiting every node exactly once: it is described by a permutation of the nodes. Its cost is the sum of the costs of its arcs. The TSP asks for the Hamiltonian tour of minimum cost.

  • Asymmetric TSP: in general \(c_{ij} \ne c_{ji}\) (one-way streets, uphill and downhill…).
  • Symmetric TSP: \(c_{ij} = c_{ji}\) for every pair.

Non-complete graphs. In a road network only some pairs of intersections are directly connected, and a tour through each node exactly once might not exist. If the graph is connected, define \(c_{ij}\) as the cost of the shortest path from \(i\) to \(j\), and we are back to a complete graph.

The TSP is NP-hard, much harder than the KP: it is strongly NP-hard, so it does not even admit pseudopolynomial algorithms like the KP's DP (unless P = NP). Many classes of heuristics exist: constructive methods, local search, metaheuristics (Variable Neighborhood Search, Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search…). We focus on the first two.

The performance ratio in a minimization problem

Maximization (KP) Minimization (TSP)
On one instance \(R_A(I) = A(I)/OPT(I) \le 1\) \(R_A(I) = A(I)/OPT(I) \ge 1\)
Worst case \(R_A = \inf_I \{A(I)/OPT(I)\} \le 1\) \(R_A = \sup_I \{A(I)/OPT(I)\} \ge 1\)
Better if higher (close to 1) lower (close to 1)

\(R_A = 2\) for a TSP means: "the tour found is never longer than twice the optimum".

The triangle inequality

It is usually assumed that

\[c_{ij} \le c_{ik} + c_{kj} \qquad \text{for all distinct } i, j, k\]

that is, going directly never costs more than passing through a third node (metric or Euclidean TSP). It avoids "strange" behaviours of algorithms and always holds when costs are shortest paths on a road network or Euclidean distances. From now on we assume it, and identify the cost of an arc with its length.

Constructive methods

They build a tour from scratch, starting from a node. They apply in the same way to symmetric and asymmetric TSPs.

Nearest neighbor

  1. Start at node 0 and follow the shortest arc leaving it.
  2. From each node reached, follow the shortest arc to a node not yet visited, until the starting point is the only choice left.
  3. Go back to node 0.
  • Complexity: each iteration scans the distances from the last node to the remaining ones (at most \(n\)), for \(n\) iterations: \(O(n^2)\).
  • Worst-case ratio: \(\tfrac12 \log_2 n + \tfrac12\), which grows with \(n\).
  • Average performance: 1.25 (25% above the optimum).

Its typical flaw shows clearly in the widget: early choices are great, but the last nodes, "forgotten" along the way, force long trips back.

Insertion methods

General scheme: start from a tour with two nodes and add the others one at a time, each where it lengthens the tour the least. Inserting \(v\) between two consecutive nodes \(i\) and \(j\) costs

\[c_{iv} + c_{vj} - c_{ij}\]

The methods differ in the choice of the first two nodes and in the rule that picks the next node.

Method Initial tour Next node \(v\) Complexity Worst-case ratio
Nearest insertion 0 and its nearest node, there and back the unvisited node closest to a node of the tour \(O(n^2)\) 2
Cheapest insertion as above the one whose insertion gives the shortest possible tour \(O(n^2 \log_2 n)\) 2
Farthest insertion 0 and its farthest node the unvisited node farthest from the tour \(O(n^2)\) \(2 \ln n + 0.16\)

In all three, the chosen node goes between the two consecutive nodes that minimize the increase in length.

Comparison. The farthest insertion ratio was proved for a random insertion algorithm: farthest insertion may have a better ratio. And it is the method that performs best in practice: nearest insertion averages 1.25, farthest insertion 1.10. The intuition: fixing the far points first draws the "skeleton" of the tour, and nearby points are then inserted cheaply.

Clarke & Wright: the savings algorithm

  1. Create \(n\) subtours \(0 \to i \to 0\), one per customer.
  2. Compute the savings \(s_{ij} = c_{i0} + c_{0j} - c_{ij}\) for \(i, j = 1, \dots, n\).
  3. Sort the savings from largest to smallest.
  4. Going down the list, merge subtours by linking \(i\) and \(j\) when possible (\(i\) and \(j\) are endpoints of two different subtours), until a single tour remains.

Replacing \(0 \to i \to 0\) and \(0 \to j \to 0\) with \(0 \to i \to j \to 0\) saves exactly \(s_{ij}\): arcs \((i, 0)\) and \((0, j)\) go, arc \((i, j)\) comes in. The same holds when merging two longer subtours \(0 \to \dots \to i \to 0\) and \(0 \to j \to \dots \to 0\).

  • Complexity: \(O(n^2 \log_2 n)\) (sorting the \(n^2\) savings dominates).
  • Worst-case ratio: not known (linear in \(\log_2 n\) for a special variant).
  • It adapts easily to more general routing problems: multiple vehicles, vehicles with limited capacity. It is the classic starting point of the Vehicle Routing Problem.

Local search

The scheme is the one of lecture 1: from a solution \(x^0\), examine its neighborhood \(N(x^0)\) and move to a better solution while one exists. In a minimization problem "better" means "with a lower objective value".

Constructive methods treat symmetric and asymmetric TSPs alike; local search for the TSP is usually developed for the symmetric TSP, on a complete undirected graph \(G = (V, E, c)\) with \(E = \{(i, j) : i, j \in V,\ i < j\}\).

k-opt moves

The neighborhood comes from a k-opt move: given a Hamiltonian cycle (a cycle visiting each vertex exactly once), remove \(k\) edges and replace them with different ones to obtain a new tour.

  • The move is already effective with small \(k\), and becomes too complicated and slow for large \(k\): searching the neighborhood costs \(O(n^k)\).
  • In practice 2-opt and 3-opt are used.
Move New tours per choice of edges Possible choices
2-opt just one \(O(n^2)\) pairs of edges
3-opt 7, of which 3 correspond to a 2-opt \(O(n^3)\) triples of edges

Why only one tour with 2-opt. Removing edges \((i, j)\) and \((p, q)\) splits the tour into two paths. To close it again without recreating the removed edges there is only one way: link \(i\) with \(p\) and \(j\) with \(q\), traversing the stretch between \(j\) and \(p\) backwards. If two edges of the tour cross, 2-opt "untangles" them, and by the triangle inequality the tour always gets shorter.

The 2-opt algorithm

  1. Generate all pairs of edges \((i, j), (p, q)\) of the tour that can be removed.
  2. For each pair compute the change \(s = c_{ip} + c_{jq} - (c_{ij} + c_{pq})\).
  3. Let \(s^*\) be the minimum \(s\).
  4. If \(s^* < 0\), make the corresponding move and go back to step 1; otherwise stop.

3-opt is identical, with triples of edges and the best saving among the 7 new tours of each triple.

The matrix \(S\). The \(s\) values fit in an \((n+1) \times (n+1)\) matrix, as large as the cost matrix, whose rows and columns are the edges of the current tour, listed from node 0 along the tour. With \(n = 6\) and tour \(0 - 3 - 2 - 4 - 1 - 5 - 6 - 0\), rows and columns are \((0,3), (3,2), (2,4), (4,1), (1,5), (5,6), (6,0)\).

  • Pair \((i, j) - (p, q)\) is a valid 2-opt only if the four nodes are all different (two adjacent edges cannot be exchanged).
  • That position holds \(c_{ip} + c_{jq} - (c_{ij} + c_{pq})\).
  • With symmetric costs the part above the diagonal suffices.
  • If there is at least one negative value, pick the most negative, make the move, recompute \(S\) and repeat; if all are nonnegative, the current tour is a local optimum.

Turn on "+ 2-opt" and step through the moves after construction: each move removes two edges (dashed) and adds two (highlighted). Notice how much nearest neighbor improves.

Conclusions

Strategy Observed average error
Constructive methods, rerun from different starting points keeping the best tour 5–7%
Composite method: constructive + 2-opt / 3-opt 2–3%
Composite method iterated from different starting points 1–2%

Today, with proper formulations and specialized branch-and-cut methods, TSP instances with thousands of nodes are solved to optimality: a famous example is the optimal tour of the 49,603 sites of the US National Register of Historic Places. The heuristics seen here stay relevant because they are simple to implement, very fast, and are the building blocks of metaheuristics for complex, real-world routing problems.

Key takeaways

  • TSP: the minimum-cost Hamiltonian tour on a complete graph; symmetric (\(c_{ij} = c_{ji}\)) or asymmetric. Strongly NP-hard.
  • In a minimization problem \(R_A = \sup_I A(I)/OPT(I) \ge 1\): the lower, the better.
  • Triangle inequality \(c_{ij} \le c_{ik} + c_{kj}\): holds for Euclidean distances and shortest paths.
  • Nearest neighbor: \(O(n^2)\), average 1.25. Insertion: nearest and cheapest with ratio 2; farthest is the best in practice (average 1.10).
  • Clarke & Wright: savings \(s_{ij} = c_{i0} + c_{0j} - c_{ij}\); extends to multiple vehicles with capacities.
  • 2-opt: remove two edges, only one way to reconnect; make the most negative move with \(s^* < 0\). Constructive + 2-opt/3-opt: average error 2–3%.
