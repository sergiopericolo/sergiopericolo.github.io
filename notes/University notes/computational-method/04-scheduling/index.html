Scheduling: priority rules on one and many machines

Lecture 4 of Computational Methods (Carlo Filippi, University of Brescia). When the scarce resource is the time of a machine, a worker or a CPU, the problem is to decide in which order to run the jobs. Often a simple sorting rule is enough: sometimes it is exact, sometimes only a heuristic, and the line between the two cases is surprising.

Roadmap:

  1. The role of scheduling — parallel machines, job shop, CPU; time as a resource.
  2. The three-field classification — how to read \(\alpha \vert \beta \vert \gamma\).
  3. Single machine — seven problems and their rules: SPT, WSPT, EDD, ERT, ECT, and the link with the TSP.
  4. Parallel machines — LPT for the makespan, SPT for the sum of completion times.

The role of scheduling

Scheduling means finding a suitable sequence of tasks (jobs) that use scarce resources, typically machinery dedicated to specific operations. More generally, every decision problem where time is a scarce resource to be allocated optimally is a scheduling problem. The models arose in production automation, but apply to logistics, project management and much more.

Example 1: parallel machines. In a mechanical plant, flexible machine tools perform different jobs (cutting, milling, turning). Each takes some time and must be assigned to one machine; going from one piece to the next requires a set-up time (tool change, tuning) that depends on the sequence. The jobs must be split among the machines and sequenced on each one to finish everything as soon as possible.

Example 2: job shop. An auto body shop has 5 workstations: Disassembling, Shaping, Hammering, Painting, Assembling, each processing one car at a time. Each damaged car needs its own sequence of operations (D → P → A; D → S → H → P → A; S → P…). Each car–operation pair is a job occupying the station for a given time. Jobs must be sequenced respecting each car's order and finishing everything in minimum time.

Example 3: CPU management. Dozens of processes share one CPU (maybe with a few cores). The operating system manages access, considering priorities, to minimize the total waiting time (weighted by priorities). It can also temporarily interrupt a process to let another one in: this is preemption.

Time as a resource: Harry and Ron at the copy machine

Harry and Ron reach a copy machine at the same time. Ron has 1 copy to make, Harry 100. Each copy takes 3 seconds. Common sense says Harry should let Ron go first. Is it right?

Order Harry finishes at Ron finishes at Total completion time
Harry first 300 303 603
Ron first 303 3 306

Yes: the short job makes the other wait little, the long job makes the other wait a lot. This is the idea behind the SPT rule.

What if Ron arrives while Harry is already copying, at time \(r > 0\)? Now what counts is the flow time, the time spent in the system (from arrival to completion):

Choice Total flow time
Ron waits for Harry to finish \(300 + (303 - r) = 603 - r\)
Harry stops and lets Ron in \((r + 3 - r) + 303 = 306\)

For every \(r < 297\) interrupting pays: preemption can be worth it.

The three-field classification

A scheduling problem is described by three fields \(\alpha \vert \beta \vert \gamma\):

Field What it describes Examples
\(\alpha\) the machine environment \(1\) (single machine), \(P\) (identical parallel machines), \(Q\) (different speeds), \(R\) (unrelated)
\(\beta\) job characteristics and constraints (empty if none) \(r_j\) (release times), \(d_j\) (due dates), \(s_{ij}\) (sequence-dependent set-ups)
\(\gamma\) the objective to minimize \(C_{\max}\), \(\sum C_j\), \(\sum w_j C_j\), \(L_{\max}\)

Notation: \(p_j\) is the processing time of job \(j\), \(C_j\) its completion time, \(C_{\max} = \max_j C_j\) the makespan (when the last job ends). So \(1 \vert r_j \vert \sum_j C_j\) reads: "one machine, jobs with release times, minimize the sum of completion times".

Single machine

\(n\) jobs to process on a single machine; job \(j\) needs \(p_j\). Find the order and the start time of each job that minimize the objective while respecting the constraints.

Problem Typical application Rule Exact?
\(1 \vert\vert C_{\max}\) The activities of one order, done by a worker in minimum time any sequence without idle time yes (trivial)
\(1 \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\) A technician assists customers; minimize total waiting SPT yes
\(1 \vert\vert \sum_j w_j C_j\) As above, with customers of different importance WSPT yes
\(1 \vert d_j \vert L_{\max}\) Orders with due dates; minimize the maximum lateness EDD yes
\(1 \vert r_j \vert C_{\max}\) A station receives jobs from the previous one at different times ERT yes
\(1 \vert r_j \vert \sum_j C_j\) Jobs arriving at different times; total waiting ECT no: NP-hard problem
\(1 \vert s_{ij} \vert C_{\max}\) Flexible station with sequence-dependent set-ups it is a TSP no: NP-hard

Each widget below also computes the optimum by trying every sequence: compare the rules, and click two bars to swap two jobs by hand.

\(1 \vert\vert C_{\max}\): the trivial case

Any sequence without idle times is optimal, with \(C_{\max} = \sum_j p_j\). With \(p = (3, 2, 1, 4)\) every order ends at 10.

\(1 \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\): the SPT rule

Now the order matters. Shortest Processing Time (SPT): process jobs by nondecreasing \(p_j\).

Claim. An SPT schedule is optimal for \(1 \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\).

Why. Take two consecutive jobs \(i\) and \(j\) starting at time \(t\). With \(i\) first, their completions add up to \((t + p_i) + (t + p_i + p_j)\); with \(j\) first, \((t + p_j) + (t + p_j + p_i)\). Other jobs do not change. Putting \(i\) before \(j\) pays if and only if \(p_i \le p_j\). A non-SPT sequence has an adjacent pair "out of order", and swapping it never hurts: we reach SPT.

With \(p = (3, 2, 1, 4)\): order 1-2-3-4 gives 24, order 2-1-4-3 gives 26, SPT (3-2-1-4) gives \(1 + 3 + 6 + 10 = \mathbf{20}\).

\(1 \vert\vert \sum_j w_j C_j\): the WSPT rule

If customers have different weights \(w_j\): Weighted Shortest Processing Time (WSPT), by nondecreasing \(p_j / w_j\). The same swap argument gives: \(i\) before \(j\) pays if \(p_i w_j \le p_j w_i\), that is \(p_i / w_i \le p_j / w_j\).

Claim. A WSPT schedule is optimal for \(1 \vert\vert \sum_j w_j C_j\).

\(j\) 1 2 3 4
\(p_j\) 3 2 1 4
\(w_j\) 10 5 2 4
\(p_j / w_j\) 0.3 0.4 0.5 1.0

WSPT gives order 1-2-3-4 with cost 107; order 2-1-4-3 costs 116, and SPT (which ignores weights) 117.

\(1 \vert d_j \vert L_{\max}\): the EDD rule

Each job has a due date \(d_j\). Its lateness is \(L_j = C_j - d_j\): positive if late, negative if early. We minimize \(L_{\max} = \max_j L_j\): finish everything on time and, if impossible, contain the worst delay. Earliest Due Date (EDD): by nondecreasing \(d_j\).

Claim. An EDD schedule is optimal for \(1 \vert d_j \vert L_{\max}\).

\(j\) 1 2 3 4 5
\(p_j\) 5 4 8 6 2
\(d_j\) 12 15 10 18 20

EDD runs 3-1-2-4-5: completions 8, 13, 17, 23, 25; lateness −2, 1, 2, 5, 5; \(L_{\max} = \mathbf{5}\). Some delay is unavoidable here, but no order does better than 5.

\(1 \vert r_j \vert C_{\max}\): the ERT rule

Now jobs arrive over time: job \(j\) is available from its release time \(r_j\). Earliest Release Time (ERT): by nondecreasing \(r_j\).

Claim. An ERT schedule is optimal for \(1 \vert r_j \vert C_{\max}\).

The rule is dynamic: when job \(j\) is released at time \(t\),

  1. if the machine is idle (\(C_{j-1} \le t\)), \(j\) starts at once and completes at \(C_j = t + p_j\);
  2. if it is busy (\(C_{j-1} > t\)), \(j\) starts when the previous one ends: \(C_j = C_{j-1} + p_j\).

With \(r_1 \le r_2 \le \dots \le r_n\), \(C_{\max} = C_n\). If the jobs are not known in advance we have an online scheduling problem, and ERT still applies: just serve jobs as they arrive.

\(j\) 1 2 3 4 5
\(p_j\) 5 4 7 7 2
\(r_j\) 9 2 12 10 5

ERT runs 2-5-1-4-3: job 2 runs from 2 to 6, job 5 from 6 to 8, then the machine sits idle until 9 (job 1 has not arrived yet), then 1 (9–14), 4 (14–21), 3 (21–28): \(C_{\max} = \mathbf{28}\).

\(1 \vert r_j \vert \sum_j C_j\): a hard problem and the ECT heuristic

It is the dynamic version of \(1 \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\), and everything changes:

Claim. \(1 \vert r_j \vert \sum_j C_j\) is NP-hard.

Meaning: any exact algorithm we know may, on some instances, take a time that grows exponentially with the instance size, and a better algorithm likely does not exist. We need a heuristic. Let \(I\) be the set of jobs already scheduled and \(C_I\) the completion of the last one (\(C_I = 0\) if \(I = \emptyset\)): a job \(j \notin I\) cannot start before \(s_j = \max\{C_I, r_j\}\).

Earliest Completion Time (ECT):

  1. \(I = \emptyset\), \(C_I = 0\).
  2. For every \(j \notin I\) compute \(\hat C_j = \max\{C_I, r_j\} + p_j\).
  3. Pick \(j^* = \arg\min_{j \notin I} \hat C_j\), the job that would finish first.
  4. Add \(j^*\) to \(I\) and set \(C_I = \hat C_{j^*}\).
  5. If jobs remain, go back to step 2.
\(j\) 1 2 3 4 5
\(p_j\) 6 3 4 5 2
\(r_j\) 7 2 18 10 3

ECT picks 2 (ends at 5), then 5 (7), 1 (13), 4 (18), 3 (22): \(\sum C_j = \mathbf{65}\). On this instance it matches the optimum; plain SPT, which ignores arrivals, does worse. Try it:

\(1 \vert s_{ij} \vert C_{\max}\): scheduling that is a TSP

A vat for dyeing fabrics: between one lot and the next it must be washed, and going from red to green needs a much more thorough wash than going from red to purple. The set-up time \(s_{ij}\) depends on the previous job \(i\) and the next job \(j\).

The sum of the \(p_j\) is constant (as in \(1 \vert\vert C_{\max}\)), so minimizing the makespan means minimizing total set-up. With six jobs, \(s_{0j}\) is the set-up from the machine's initial state to job \(j\), and \(s_{j0}\) the one leaving it in the desired state at the end (0 if it can be left as is):

\(s_{ij}\) 0 1 2 3 4 5 6
0 – 7.6 8.9 16.9 9.6 6.1 7.6
1 0 – 9.6 10.9 12.9 22.6 16.1
2 0 8.6 – 3.8 7.5 14.3 9
3 0 6.9 5.8 – 8.7 11.7 13.3
4 0 12.9 10.5 6.7 – 6 10.4
5 0 19.6 17.3 10.7 9 – 15.5
6 0 15.1 9 14.3 13.4 19.5 –

On the complete directed graph with nodes \(\{0, \dots, 6\}\) and costs \(s_{ij}\), a sequence is a Hamiltonian tour whose "depot" 0 is the initial and final state of the machine: the machine is a "vehicle" that "visits" the jobs. \(1 \vert s_{ij} \vert C_{\max}\) is an asymmetric TSP. The heuristics of lecture 3 apply:

  • nearest neighbor: 0 → 5 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 6 → 0, total set-up \(6.1 + 9 + 6.7 + 5.8 + 8.6 + 16.1 + 0 = 52.3\);
  • optimum: 0 → 5 → 4 → 6 → 2 → 3 → 1 → 0, total set-up \(6.1 + 9 + 10.4 + 9 + 3.8 + 6.9 + 0 = \mathbf{45.2}\).

Nearest neighbor loses 16%: once at job 1, only job 6 is left, and that changeover costs 16.1.

Parallel machines

\(n\) jobs on \(m\) parallel machines, i.e. general purpose: every machine can process every job. If they are identical, job \(j\) takes \(p_j\) on any machine; otherwise the time \(p_{ij}\) depends on machine \(i\). Each job must be assigned to a machine, and order and start times decided on each. The simplest cases are \(P \vert\vert C_{\max}\) and \(P \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\).

In the widgets, jobs are assigned in the order of the rule, each one to the first machine that becomes free.

\(P \vert\vert C_{\max}\): the LPT rule

The activities of one customer order are spread over identical machines to finish as soon as possible. Trivial with one machine, the problem becomes NP-hard already with two: it is, in essence, splitting numbers into groups with sums as equal as possible. An integer programming model can be written (essentially the same for \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R \vert\vert C_{\max}\)), but simple heuristics work well.

Longest Processing Time (LPT):

  1. Sort jobs by nonincreasing processing time.
  2. Assign them one at a time to the first machine that becomes free (break ties arbitrarily).

The idea: long jobs must be placed early, and short ones, at the end, serve to "level" the loads. The LPT solution can then be improved with a local search.

\(j\) 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
\(p_j\) 12 5 7 7 9 4 6

On 3 machines, LPT takes 1 (12), 5 (9), 3 (7), 4 (7), 7 (6), 2 (5), 6 (4) and gets M1 = {1, 2} = 17, M2 = {5, 7} = 15, M3 = {3, 4, 6} = 18: \(C_{\max} = 18\). But \(\sum p_j = 50\) and \(50/3 \approx 16.7\), so no solution goes below 17, and 17 is reachable (for instance {1, 2}, {5, 3}, {4, 7, 6}): LPT is one unit from the optimum. Try swapping two bars to reach 17.

\(P \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\): SPT, still exact

With the sum of completion times, parallel machines do not make the problem harder. SPT: sort jobs by nondecreasing \(p_j\) and assign each to the first free machine.

Claim. An SPT schedule is optimal for \(P \vert\vert \sum_j C_j\).

\(j\) 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
\(p_j\) 6 8 5 7 10 7 4

On 3 machines SPT takes 7 (4), 3 (5), 1 (6), 4 (7), 6 (7), 2 (8), 5 (10): M1 = 7, 4, 5 (completions 4, 11, 21); M2 = 3, 6 (5, 12); M3 = 1, 2 (6, 14). \(\sum C_j = \mathbf{73}\).

A counterintuitive complexity

Machines \(C_{\max}\) \(\sum_j C_j\) \(\sum_j w_j C_j\)
1 trivial easy: SPT (exact) easy: WSPT (exact)
P NP-hard: LPT (heuristic) easy: SPT (exact) NP-hard

Adding machines makes the makespan hard but leaves the sum of completions easy; adding weights on parallel machines makes that hard too.

Conclusions

The analysis focused on constructive methods, mostly relying on a suitable ordering of the jobs. They are sometimes exact (for "easy" problems) and sometimes heuristic (for "hard" ones). For hard problems a range of more sophisticated algorithms exists, starting with local search, too specific and complex to be analysed here.

Key takeaways

  • Notation \(\alpha \vert \beta \vert \gamma\): machines, job constraints, objective. \(C_j\) completion, \(C_{\max}\) makespan, \(L_j = C_j - d_j\) lateness.
  • Single machine: SPT optimal for \(\sum C_j\), WSPT (\(p_j/w_j\)) for \(\sum w_j C_j\), EDD for \(L_{\max}\), ERT for \(C_{\max}\) with releases. Proofs swap adjacent jobs.
  • \(1 \vert r_j \vert \sum C_j\) is NP-hard: ECT heuristic (the job that would finish first).
  • \(1 \vert s_{ij} \vert C_{\max}\) is a TSP: jobs are cities, set-ups are distances.
  • Parallel machines: LPT heuristic for \(P \vert\vert C_{\max}\) (NP-hard), SPT exact for \(P \vert\vert \sum C_j\).
