Modern Portfolio Theory

Chapter 2 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). How to combine risky assets so that, for a given risk, the expected return is as high as possible (Markowitz, 1952).

Roadmap:

  1. Returns and risk — prices, returns, PRI vs. TRI, volatility.
  2. Decision criteria — state-by-state dominance, mean–variance dominance, Sharpe ratio.
  3. The GMV portfolio — two and three risky assets, correlation and diversification.
  4. Markowitz with N risky assets — the portfolio frontier, two-fund separation.
  5. Adding a riskless asset — CML, tangency portfolio, one-fund theorem.
  6. Theory meets practice — five DJIA stocks, 2000–2022.

Why finance and models

Finance is the economy's circulatory system: it channels savings to investment, fosters growth and is a tool for 21st-century problems (climate, inequality). Models rationalise complex choices and guide decisions under uncertainty. Their history: theory-driven in the 1950s–60s, then tested on data, today evidence-based.

Prices, returns and risk

With no dividends, the price evolves as \(P_t = P_{t-1}(1 + R_t)\):

\[1 + R_t = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} \quad\text{(gross)}, \qquad R_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \quad\text{(net)}\]

PRI vs. TRI. A price return index captures only capital gains; a total return index adds dividends \(D_t\):

\[R_t^{PRI} = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}, \qquad R_t^{TRI} = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1} + D_t}{P_{t-1}}\]

For the same security \(R^{TRI} \ge R^{PRI}\); TRI is today's industry standard.

Volatility = standard deviation of returns, the most common risk measure:

\[\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} (R_t - \bar R)^2}\]

Chain to remember: prices → returns → risk.

Decision criteria under risk

Mean–variance scenarios

Compare portfolio 1 and portfolio 2:

Scenario Condition Conclusion
A \(E(\tilde r_1) > E(\tilde r_2)\) and \(\sigma_1 \le \sigma_2\) 1 mean–variance dominates 2
B \(E(\tilde r_1) \ge E(\tilde r_2)\) and \(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2\) No clear preference
C \(E(\tilde r_1) \le E(\tilde r_2)\) and \(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2\) 1 is inefficient

An efficient portfolio gives the highest return for its risk, or the lowest risk for its return.

A numerical example

Three stocks cost 100 today; next year Good or Bad state, each with probability 50%.

Good Bad \(E(R)\) \(\sigma(R)\)
Stock 1 120 (+20%) 105 (+5%) 12.5% 7.5%
Stock 2 160 (+60%) 50 (−50%) 5.0% 55.0%
Stock 3 160 (+60%) 105 (+5%) 32.5% 27.5%

\[E(R_1) = 0.5(20\%) + 0.5(5\%) = 12.5\%, \qquad \sigma(R_1) = \sqrt{0.5(20 - 12.5)^2 + 0.5(5 - 12.5)^2} = 7.5\%\]

  • State-by-state dominance: Stock 3 pays at least as much as the others in every state → always preferred.
  • Mean–variance dominance: Stock 1 dominates Stock 2. But Stock 3 does not dominate Stock 1 (more risk), even though it dominates state by state — a limit of mean–variance.

When neither criterion ranks two assets, use the Sharpe ratio (here \(r_f = 0\)):

\[SR = \frac{E(R) - r_f}{\sigma(R)}\]

Good Bad \(E(R)\) \(\sigma(R)\) Sharpe
Stock 4 5% 3% 4.0% 1.0% 4.00
Stock 5 8% 2% 5.0% 3.0% 1.67

Sharpe prefers Stock 4, but it assumes a linear trade-off: doubling risk is fine if return doubles. Not every risk-averse investor agrees.

Criterion Strength Limit
State-by-state Most robust Rarely applicable
Mean–variance Widely applicable Can mislead, does not rank everything
Sharpe ratio Always ranks Embeds a specific risk tolerance

The gold standard remains expected utility (Chapter 1): it uses the investor's own preferences.

The GMV portfolio: two risky assets

With weights \(w_1\) and \(w_2 = 1 - w_1\):

\[\mu_p = w_1\mu_1 + w_2\mu_2, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\sigma_{1,2}, \qquad \sigma_{1,2} = \rho_{1,2}\sigma_1\sigma_2\]

Minimising \(\sigma_p^2\) subject to \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\) gives the global minimum variance portfolio:

\[w_{1,gmv} = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_{1,2}}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_{1,2}}, \qquad \sigma_{p,gmv}^2 = \frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1 - \rho_{1,2}^2)}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho_{1,2}\sigma_1\sigma_2}\]

The role of correlation

  • \(\rho = -1\): perfect hedge. \(\sigma_p^2 = (w_a(\sigma_a + \sigma_b) - \sigma_b)^2 = 0\) for \[w_a = \frac{\sigma_b}{\sigma_a + \sigma_b}, \quad w_b = \frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_a + \sigma_b}, \quad E(r_{ph}) = \frac{\sigma_b E(\tilde r_a) + \sigma_a E(\tilde r_b)}{\sigma_a + \sigma_b}\] The frontier has maximum concavity (two straight lines meeting on the \(\mu\) axis).
  • \(\rho = +1\): the frontier is a straight line, no diversification benefit between the two assets. A zero-risk portfolio still exists with a short position: \[w_a = -\frac{\sigma_b}{\sigma_a - \sigma_b}, \quad w_b = \frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_a - \sigma_b}\]
  • \(\rho = 0\): \(\sigma_{p,gmv}^2 = \dfrac{\sigma_a^2\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2}\), below both variances.
  • \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\): the frontier is concave; the higher \(\rho\), the closer it gets to the straight line, the smaller the benefit.

Move \(\rho\) and watch the frontier bend between the two dotted extremes (\(\rho = -1\) and \(\rho = +1\)). At \(\rho = -1\) the GMV reaches \(\sigma = 0\).

More assets: the insurance principle

\[\sigma_p^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2\sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j \ne i} w_iw_j\sigma_{ij}\]

With uncorrelated assets, equal variances \(\sigma^2\) and equal weights \(1/N\): \(\sigma_p^2 = \sigma^2 / N \to 0\) as \(N \to \infty\). Insurers rely on this: bad news for some firms is offset by good news for others. With an average correlation \(\rho > 0\):

\[\sigma_p^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{N} + \Big(1 - \frac1N\Big)\rho\,\sigma^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \rho\,\sigma^2\]

Idiosyncratic risk disappears; systematic risk (market crashes) does not.

Three risky assets and matrix notation

\[\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + w_3^2\sigma_3^2 + 2w_1w_2\sigma_{1,2} + 2w_1w_3\sigma_{1,3} + 2w_2w_3\sigma_{2,3}\]

Three variances and six covariances: use vectors. With \(\mathbf w = (w_1, w_2, w_3)'\), \(\boldsymbol\mu = E[\tilde{\mathbf r}]\) and the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\):

\[\mu_p = \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\mu, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w\]

GMV in matrix form. \(\min_{\mathbf w} \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w\) s.t. \(\mathbf w'\mathbf 1 = 1\). Lagrangian \(\Lambda = \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w + \lambda(1 - \mathbf w'\mathbf 1)\):

  1. FOC: \(2\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w - \lambda\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf w = \tfrac12\lambda\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1\).
  2. Constraint: \(\lambda = 2 / (\mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)\).
  3. Substitute:

\[\mathbf w_{gmv} = \frac{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}{\mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}\]

Equivalently the four FOCs form the linear system \(\begin{pmatrix} 2\boldsymbol\Sigma & \mathbf 1 \\ \mathbf 1' & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf w \\ \lambda\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\).

Markowitz mean–variance with N risky assets

Assumptions: frictionless markets (no costs, taxes, spreads), unlimited trading and short sales, infinitely divisible assets, equal access to information. Useful simplifications, not reality.

Problem. For a target return \(\mu_p\):

\[\min_{\mathbf w} \tfrac12\mathbf w'\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\mu = \mu_p, \;\; \mathbf w'\mathbf 1 = 1\]

\[\Lambda = \tfrac12\mathbf w'\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w + \lambda(\mu_p - \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\mu) + \psi(1 - \mathbf w'\mathbf 1)\]

Solution.

  1. FOC in \(\mathbf w\): \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w - \lambda\boldsymbol\mu - \psi\mathbf 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf w = \lambda\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu + \psi\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1\).
  2. Define the scalars \(A = \boldsymbol\mu'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\), \(B = \boldsymbol\mu'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1\), \(C = \mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1\), \(\Delta = AC - B^2\).
  3. The constraints give \(\mu_p = A\lambda + B\psi\) and \(1 = B\lambda + C\psi\), so \[\lambda = \frac{C\mu_p - B}{\Delta}, \qquad \psi = \frac{A - B\mu_p}{\Delta}\]
  4. Substituting:

\[\mathbf w = \mathbf g + \mathbf h\,\mu_p, \qquad \mathbf g = \frac{1}{\Delta}\big[A\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1 - B\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu\big], \qquad \mathbf h = \frac1\Delta\big[C\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu - B\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1\big]\]

\(\mathbf g\) and \(\mathbf g + \mathbf h\) are the frontier portfolios for \(\mu_p = 0\) and \(\mu_p = 1\).

The portfolio frontier

\[\sigma_p^2 = \frac1\Delta\big(C\mu_p^2 - 2B\mu_p + A\big) = \frac{C}{\Delta}\Big(\mu_p - \frac BC\Big)^2 + \frac1C\]

A hyperbola in \((\sigma, \mu)\), i.e. \(\mu_p = \frac BC \pm \sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{C}\big(\sigma_p^2 - \frac1C\big)}\). Its vertex is the GMV:

\[\mu_{gmv} = \frac BC, \qquad \sigma_{gmv}^2 = \frac1C, \qquad \mathbf w_{gmv} = \frac{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}{\mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}\]

(at the GMV, \(\lambda = 0\) and \(\psi = 1/C\)).

  • The efficient frontier is the upper branch: portfolios with \(\mu_p \ge B/C\). The lower branch is inefficient.
  • The frontier is convex: \(\alpha\mathbf w_k + (1-\alpha)\mathbf w_q = \mathbf g + \mathbf h[\alpha\mu_k + (1-\alpha)\mu_q]\) is again a frontier portfolio.
  • If every investor holds an efficient portfolio, the market portfolio (their weighted sum) is efficient too.

Two-fund separation

Any two distinct efficient portfolios span the whole frontier: for any efficient \(p\) there is \(\alpha \in \mathbb R\) with

\[\mathbf w_p = \alpha\mathbf w_1 + (1-\alpha)\mathbf w_2, \qquad \mu_p = \alpha\mu_1 + (1-\alpha)\mu_2\]

because \(\mathbf g + \mathbf h[\alpha\mu_1 + (1-\alpha)\mu_2] = \alpha(\mathbf g + \mathbf h\mu_1) + (1-\alpha)(\mathbf g + \mathbf h\mu_2)\). Two mutual funds are enough.

Adding a riskless asset

A riskless asset returns \(r_f\) with zero variance, and \(r_f < B/C\). The risky weights \(\mathbf w\) no longer sum to 1; \(w_f = 1 - \mathbf w'\mathbf 1\) (\(w_f < 0\) = borrowing, \(w_f > 0\) = lending at \(r_f\)).

\[\mu_p = \mathbf w'\boldsymbol\mu + (1 - \mathbf w'\mathbf 1)r_f = r_f + \mathbf w'(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\]

Problem: \(\min \tfrac12\mathbf w'\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf w\) s.t. \(r_f + \mathbf w'(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f) = \mu_p\).

  1. FOC: \(\mathbf w = \lambda\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\).
  2. Constraint: \(\lambda = (\mu_p - r_f)/H\) with \(H = (\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\).

\[\mathbf w = \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\,\frac{\mu_p - r_f}{H}\]

The direction \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\) is fixed; \(\mu_p\) only scales it. The variance is \(\sigma_p^2 = (\mu_p - r_f)^2/H\), so the frontier becomes a straight line, the Capital Market Line:

\[\mu_p = r_f + \sqrt H\,\sigma_p\]

Only the upper half is efficient. The slope \(\sqrt H\) is the highest attainable Sharpe ratio.

Tangency portfolio

The CML touches the risky frontier at the tangency portfolio \(T\), which holds only risky assets (impose \(\mathbf w'\mathbf 1 = 1\)):

\[\mathbf w_T = \frac{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)}{\mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)}, \qquad \sigma_T = \frac{\sqrt H}{\Upsilon}, \qquad \mu_T = r_f + \frac{H}{\Upsilon}, \qquad \Upsilon = \mathbf 1'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\]

Same shape as \(\mathbf w_{gmv}\), with excess returns in place of \(\mathbf 1\). If \(r_f > \mu_{gmv}\) the Sharpe ratio turns negative: in a crisis \(T\) may be shorted and the proceeds put in T-bills.

Mixing \(\alpha\) in the riskless asset and \(1 - \alpha\) in \(T\):

\[\mu_p = \alpha r_f + (1-\alpha)\mu_T, \quad \sigma_p = (1-\alpha)\sigma_T \;\Rightarrow\; \mu_p = r_f + \frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T}\sigma_p\]

  • \(0 \le \alpha \le 1\): lend, between \(r_f\) and \(T\).
  • \(\alpha < 0\): borrow at \(r_f\), leveraged beyond \(T\).
  • Any other risky portfolio \(k\) gives a flatter line, \(\frac{\mu_k - r_f}{\sigma_k} < \frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T}\): inefficient.

Move \(r_f\): the CML pivots and \(T\) slides along the frontier.

One-fund theorem

All efficient portfolios combine one riskless asset and one risky fund, \(T\). With mean–variance preferences and homogeneous expectations, everyone holds the same \(T\), which must then be the market portfolio — the starting point of the CAPM (Chapter 3).

Key takeaways

  • Returns: \(R_t = P_t/P_{t-1} - 1\); TRI includes dividends; risk = volatility \(\sigma\).
  • Criteria: state-by-state (robust, rare) → mean–variance (can mislead) → Sharpe (linear trade-off) → expected utility (gold standard).
  • Two assets: \(w_{1,gmv} = (\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_{12})/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_{12})\); the lower \(\rho\), the bigger the diversification gain; \(\rho = -1\) → perfect hedge.
  • Diversification kills idiosyncratic risk, not systematic risk.
  • N assets: \(\mathbf w = \mathbf g + \mathbf h\mu_p\); frontier \(\sigma_p^2 = \frac C\Delta(\mu_p - \frac BC)^2 + \frac1C\); GMV at \((1/\sqrt C, B/C)\).
  • Two-fund separation: two efficient portfolios span the frontier.
  • With \(r_f\): CML \(\mu_p = r_f + \sqrt H\sigma_p\), tangency \(\mathbf w_T \propto \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf 1 r_f)\); one-fund theorem.
  • In practice: extreme weights, estimation error → constraints, Black–Litterman.
