Empirical Analysis of the CAPM

en

Empirical Analysis of the CAPM

Chapter 4 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). Bringing the CAPM to the data: estimate alpha and beta with a time-series regression, test whether alpha is zero, then let alpha and beta move over time.

Roadmap:

  1. Univariate regression analysis — the market model, OLS, Jensen's alpha and its t-test.
  2. Static test — five DJIA stocks, 2000–2022.
  3. Dynamic analysis — rolling-window alphas and betas.

Foundations

  • Rational expectations: long-run realised returns ≈ expected returns, so averages of past returns proxy for \(E[\cdot]\).
  • Risk–return trade-off: only systematic risk is priced in ideal markets; frictions (costs, constraints) distort premia.
  • No universal model: effectiveness depends on the asset and context.
  • Standard assumptions: risk aversion, single-period utility maximisation, homogeneous beliefs, frictionless markets, unlimited borrowing and lending at \(r_f\).

In excess returns \(\tilde z = \tilde r - r_f\) the CAPM reads \(E(\tilde z_i) = \beta_{i,M}E(\tilde z_M)\): no intercept.

Early evidence

  • Non-zero alphas (Merton and Scholes, 1972): low-beta stocks earn positive alpha.
  • Cross-sectional regressions show intercept too high, slope too flat (Black, Jensen and Scholes, 1972).
  • Black's two-factor (zero-beta) version: \(E(\tilde z_i) = (1 - \beta_i)E(\tilde z_l) + \beta_{i,M}E(\tilde z_M)\), where \(\tilde z_l\) is a zero-beta portfolio.

History. Before the CAPM the cost of capital came from dividend yield plus growth (Gordon–Shapiro, \(r = D/P + g\)), with no explicit risk adjustment. Markowitz (1952) laid the groundwork; the CAPM brought risk into pricing and made it testable. It is still the dominant cost-of-capital model (Welch, 2008: 75% of academics endorse it).

The market model

Excess return \(z_{i,t} = r_{i,t} - r_{f,t}\) with \(r_{i,t} = \dfrac{p_{i,t} + d_{i,t} - p_{i,t-1}}{p_{i,t-1}}\):

\[z_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}z_{M,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \qquad t = 1, \dots, T\]

  • \(\alpha_i\): pricing error; zero if the CAPM holds.
  • \(\beta_{i,M}\): sensitivity to the market excess return.
  • \(\varepsilon_{i,t}\): idiosyncratic shock.
  • Market proxy: broad indices (S&P 500, FTSE All-Share), since the true \(M\) is unobservable.

Assumptions on the errors: \(E[\varepsilon_{i,t} \mid z_{M,t}] = 0\); \(\mathrm{Cov}[\varepsilon_{i,t}, z_{M,t}] = 0\); homoskedasticity \(\mathrm{Var}[\varepsilon_{i,t} \mid z_{M,t}] = \sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2\); no autocorrelation. They make OLS consistent and efficient and validate inference on \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_{i,M}\).

Population parameters

\[\beta_{i,M} = \frac{\sigma_{i,M}}{\sigma_M^2}, \qquad \alpha_i = \mu_i - \beta_{i,M}\mu_M\]

Multivariate version \(\mathbf z_t = \boldsymbol\alpha + \boldsymbol\beta z_{M,t} + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t\): joint estimation (SUR, GLS) gains power and uses cross-asset dependence, at the cost of complexity. The book uses univariate regressions.

OLS

Minimise the sum of squared residuals \(SSR = \sum_{t=1}^{T}(z_{i,t} - \alpha - \beta z_{m,t})^2\).

  1. \(\partial SSR / \partial\alpha = 0 \Rightarrow \hat\alpha = \bar z_i - \hat\beta\bar z_m\).
  2. \(\partial SSR / \partial\beta = 0 \Rightarrow\)

\[\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_t (z_{i,t} - \bar z_i)(z_{m,t} - \bar z_m)}{\sum_t (z_{m,t} - \bar z_m)^2} = \frac{\sum_t z_{i,t}z_{m,t} - T\bar z_i\bar z_m}{\sum_t z_{m,t}^2 - T\bar z_m^2}, \qquad \hat\alpha = \bar z_i - \hat\beta\bar z_m\]

Jensen's alpha

\(\hat\alpha_i\) is the part of the average return not explained by the market (Jensen, 1968), the classic measure of a manager's skill:

  • \(\hat\alpha_i > 0\): outperforms the CAPM benchmark on average.
  • \(\hat\alpha_i < 0\): underperforms.

Testing it. \(H_0: \alpha_i = 0\) (the CAPM explains the returns) vs. \(H_1: \alpha_i \ne 0\). The appraisal ratio is the t-statistic

\[t_{\hat\alpha_i} = \frac{\hat\alpha_i}{\hat\sigma_{\hat\alpha_i}}, \qquad \hat\sigma_{\hat\alpha_i}^2 = \frac1T\left(1 + \frac{\bar z_M^2}{\hat\sigma_M^2}\right)\hat s_{\varepsilon_i}^2, \qquad \hat s_{\varepsilon_i}^2 = \frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat\varepsilon_{i,t}^2\]

Under joint normality and \(H_0\), \(t_{\hat\alpha_i} \sim t_{T-2}\). The term \(\bar z_M^2/\hat\sigma_M^2\) is the squared Sharpe ratio of the market: alpha is estimated less precisely when the market's mean is large relative to its volatility.

Play with the true parameters. With much idiosyncratic noise or a short sample, even a true alpha of 0.3% per month is often not significant; a new sample changes the estimates.

Alphas. Only P&G has a significant alpha (10% level, about 0.5% per month): abnormal returns beyond market risk. For the other four we cannot reject \(\alpha_i = 0\) — consistent with the CAPM, as is typical for large caps.

Betas. All positive and significant at 1%. AmEx has \(\beta > 1\) (aggressive); the others \(\beta < 1\) (defensive). If the market rises 1%, the stock's expected excess return changes by \(\beta \times 1\%\).

Fit. The market explains 42% of AmEx's variation, about 36–38% for 3M and IBM, only 5.5% for P&G and 8.7% for Merck.

Limits. Five large, liquid, U.S. stocks from one index. A serious test needs many stocks of different size and liquidity, international markets and different market conditions.

Dynamic analysis: time-varying alpha and beta

The static CAPM assumes constant \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_i\). In reality they move with business models and leverage, the business cycle and volatility, managerial skill, regulation.

Rolling-window regressions (windows of 25, 40 or 60 months): estimate on \(t = 1, \dots, 40\), then \(t = 2, \dots, 41\), …, up to \(t = T-39, \dots, T\). Each window gives one \((\hat\alpha_{i,t}, \hat\beta_{i,t})\).

Uses: dynamic risk assessment and allocation, risk evaluation in volatile markets, performance evaluation (skill vs. timing). Evidence: betas rise in market stress; alphas vary, especially for active managers.

Below, the true beta jumps from 1.1 to 2.2 during a crisis, then falls. A short window tracks the jump quickly but is noisy; a long window is smooth but lags and blurs the crisis.

What the DJIA stocks show (40-month windows)

Stock Rolling alpha Rolling beta
P&G Positive 2003–05 and 2017–22, ≈ 0 in between From negative (counter-cyclical) pre-2007 to 0.6–0.7, then ≈ 0.5
3M Positive 2003–05, ≈ 0 2006–18, negative after 2018 0.5–1 early, ≈ 1 after 2008
IBM Negative 2004–06, positive 2007–13, negative after 2014 > 1 in 2003–08, 0.5–1 in 2009–17, > 1 after 2017
Merck Negative 2003–06, ≈ 0 until 2018, positive after Always between 0 and 1
AmEx Very volatile throughout 1–1.5, below 1 in 2006–07, above 2 in 2008–09, above 1 after 2012

Each firm has its own pattern of out- and underperformance: factors beyond systematic risk matter. Betas vary too, but less than alphas.

Limits of rolling estimates

  • Ex post: they describe the past, with no ex-ante predictive power.
  • Window size: no universal rule; enough data for precision vs. responsiveness; should match the investor's horizon.
  • Frequency: daily, weekly, monthly or annual data change the right window.
  • Hence a subjective trade-off between stability and adaptability.

Key takeaways

  • Market model: \(z_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}z_{M,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}\); the CAPM implies \(\alpha_i = 0\).
  • OLS: \(\hat\beta = \widehat{\mathrm{cov}}(z_i, z_m)/\widehat{\mathrm{var}}(z_m)\), \(\hat\alpha = \bar z_i - \hat\beta\bar z_m\).
  • Jensen's alpha measures performance; test it with \(t_{\hat\alpha} = \hat\alpha/\hat\sigma_{\hat\alpha} \sim t_{T-2}\).
  • DJIA 2000–22: only P&G has a (weakly) significant alpha; AmEx aggressive (\(\beta = 1.29\)), others defensive.
  • Rolling windows reveal time-varying alphas and betas (betas spike in crises) but are ex post and window-dependent.
