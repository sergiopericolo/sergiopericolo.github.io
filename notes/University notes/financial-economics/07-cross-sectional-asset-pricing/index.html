en

Empirical Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing

Chapter 7 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). Time-series regressions (Chapters 4 and 6) ask whether alphas are zero. Cross-sectional tests ask the central question directly: why do different assets earn different average returns, and what is the price of each risk?

Roadmap:

  1. Theory vs. empirics — what cross-sectional tests are for.
  2. The Fama–MacBeth two-step procedure — betas first, risk premia second.
  3. Econometric issues — errors-in-variables (Shanken), heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation (Newey–West).
  4. Applications — is economic policy uncertainty priced? Is temperature risk priced?

Theory vs. empirics

Daily return fluctuations mostly reflect news. Long-run differences in average returns need a model of how risk, market conditions and behaviour shape returns.

  • Theory produces testable predictions about the risk–return relation.
  • Empirics documents stylised facts (anomalies) the theory cannot explain (Campbell, 2000). Candidate risk factors are macro (inflation, industrial production, unemployment) or financial (size, volatility, liquidity).

Standard test: the two-step regressions of Fama and MacBeth (1973), used today for macro uncertainty, climate risk and more.

The Fama–MacBeth procedure

Step 1 — time series: estimate the betas. For each asset or portfolio \(n\):

\[r_{n,t} = \alpha_n + \beta_{n,f_1}f_{1,t} + \dots + \beta_{n,f_M}f_{M,t} + \varepsilon_{n,t}\]

\(\beta_{n,f_m}\) is the loading of \(n\) on factor \(m\).

Step 2 — cross section: estimate the risk premia. At each date \(t\), regress the returns of all \(N\) assets on their estimated betas:

\[r_{n,t} = \lambda_{t,0} + \lambda_{t,1}\hat\beta_{n,f_1} + \dots + \lambda_{t,M}\hat\beta_{n,f_M} + \nu_{n,t}\]

Repeat for \(t = 1, \dots, T\) and average:

\[\bar\lambda_m = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat\lambda_{t,m}, \qquad t(\bar\lambda_m) = \frac{\bar\lambda_m}{\hat\sigma(\hat\lambda_{t,m})/\sqrt T}\]

\(\bar\lambda_m\) is the price of risk of factor \(m\): the extra average return per unit of beta.

Alternative: average-return regression. One single cross-sectional regression on average returns,

\[E[R_n] = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1\hat\beta_{n,f_1} + \dots + \lambda_M\hat\beta_{n,f_M} + \nu_n\]

gives the same \(\hat\lambda_m\) but different standard errors; simpler, but inference is more sensitive to the specification.

The simulation has 25 portfolios with one priced factor. Left: the cross-sectional relation (step 2 on averages). Right: the 276 monthly slopes \(\lambda_t\) — very dispersed, so the premium is only significant when the sample is long or the premium large. Note that \(\hat\lambda\) tracks the factor's sample mean (the premium actually realised), not the true \(\lambda\): press "new sample" a few times and you will sometimes find no premium at all.

Econometric issues

  • Errors-in-variables: the second-step regressors \(\hat\beta\) are estimates.
  • Cross-sectional heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation of the residuals.
  • The intercept: imposing \(\lambda_0 = 0\) or not changes the results (with excess returns the theory says \(\lambda_0 = 0\)).
  • Omitted factors bias the estimated premia.

Errors-in-variables: the Shanken correction

The naive OLS variance of \(\hat\lambda\) ignores that the betas are estimated:

\[\sigma^2_{OLS}(\hat\lambda) = \frac1T(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\beta'E(\epsilon\epsilon')\beta(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\]

Shanken (1992) inflates it to account for the first step:

\[\sigma^2_{Shanken}(\hat\lambda) = \frac1T\Big[(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\beta'E(\epsilon\epsilon')\beta(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\big(1 + \lambda'E(ff')^{-1}\lambda\big) + \Sigma_f\Big]\]

The factor \((1 + \lambda'E(ff')^{-1}\lambda)\) is the first-step correction: the higher the factor's squared Sharpe ratio, the bigger the inflation. Shanken t-statistics are therefore smaller.

Heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation: Newey–West

\[\sigma^2_{NW}(\hat\lambda) = (\beta'\beta)^{-1}\beta'\Omega\beta(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\]

with

\[\beta'\Omega\beta = \frac{n}{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_i^2\beta_i'\beta_i + \frac{n}{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\Big(1 - \frac{i}{h+1}\Big)\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\nu_j\nu_{j-1}\big(\beta_j'\beta_{j-1} + \beta_{j-1}'\beta_j\big)\]

The Bartlett weights \(1 - i/(h+1)\) down-weight distant lags. Statistical packages implement these HAC standard errors.

Applications: which risks are priced?

Two strands of research:

  • Firm-level factors: asset growth (Cooper et al., 2008); the Fama–French five-factor model adds investment and profitability (2015, 2016).
  • Macro factors: investment-specific shocks (Garlappi and Song, 2017: not robust for 1964–2012, partial long-run role); macro disagreement from forecast dispersion (Li, 2015: high-macro-beta stocks earn less after high-disagreement periods); policy uncertainty and climate risk, both with evidence of positive premia.

Design: 25 Fama–French size × B/M portfolios, Jan 2000 – Dec 2022, Fama–MacBeth two-step regressions. An educational demonstration of the method.

Is economic policy uncertainty priced?

Hypothesis: higher EPU raises perceived risk, so investors demand compensation for exposure to EPU shocks. The EPU index (Baker, Bloom and Davis, 2016) counts newspaper articles on policy uncertainty; it spikes at 9/11 (2001), Lehman (2008), COVID-19 (2020).

Step 1, for each portfolio:

\[R_{n,t} - r_{f,t} = \alpha_n + \beta_{n,M}(R_{M,t} - r_{f,t}) + \beta_{n,\Delta C}\Delta C_t + \beta_{n,\Delta EPU}\Delta EPU_t + \varepsilon_{n,t}\]

Step 2 on average returns, \(E[R_n - r_f] = \lambda_M\hat\beta_{n,M} + \lambda_{\Delta C}\hat\beta_{n,\Delta C} + \lambda_{\Delta EPU}\hat\beta_{n,\Delta EPU} + \nu_n\), or month by month and averaged.

Is temperature risk priced?

U.S. average annual temperature rose from about 8.5°C to about 11°C over 1901–2021 (World Bank). Extreme weather destroys physical capital and moves asset prices, so temperature is a natural candidate risk factor.

Same method, with temperature changes \(\Delta T\) in place of EPU:

\[R_{n,t} - r_{f,t} = \alpha_n + \beta_{n,M}(R_{M,t} - r_{f,t}) + \beta_{n,\Delta C}\Delta C_t + \beta_{n,\Delta T}\Delta T_t + \varepsilon_{n,t}\]

  • Positive temperature exposure for 22 of 25 portfolios.

  • Consistent with the climate-finance literature: exposure to rising temperatures commands compensation.

Implications. Investors: temperature risk is priced → climate-resilient strategies, better risk management. Policymakers: evidence of climate-related financial risk, a basis for regulation and stability analysis. Limits: preliminary, few portfolios, U.S. only; future work should use more portfolios, countries, sectors and climate measures.

Key takeaways

  • Cross-sectional question: why do average returns differ across assets? Answer: different exposures (betas) times prices of risk (\(\lambda\)).
  • Fama–MacBeth: (1) time-series regressions → \(\hat\beta_n\); (2) cross-sectional regressions each month → \(\hat\lambda_t\); average → \(\bar\lambda\), t-stat from the spread of \(\hat\lambda_t\).
  • Average-return regression: same \(\hat\lambda\), different standard errors.
  • Betas are estimated → Shanken correction (smaller t-stats); residuals are heteroskedastic/autocorrelated → Newey–West HAC.
  • EPU: positive premium, not robust to errors-in-variables. Temperature: positive premium (\(0.231\)), more robust; 22/25 portfolios exposed, small caps most.
