The Black–Litterman Model

en

The Black–Litterman Model

Chapter 8 of Essentials of Financial Economics (Donadelli, Costola, Gufler). Markowitz needs expected returns as inputs, and historical estimates produce extreme, unstable weights (Chapter 2). Black and Litterman (1990, 1992) start instead from market equilibrium and let each investor tilt it with their own views, combined by Bayes' rule.

Roadmap:

  1. Motivation — homogeneous vs. heterogeneous investors.
  2. Market equilibrium — reverse optimisation, \(\Pi = \gamma\Sigma\mathbf w_{mkt}\), the role of \(\tau\).
  3. The investor's views — \(\mathbf P\), \(\mathbf Q\), \(\boldsymbol\Omega\).
  4. The master formula — Bayesian posterior of expected returns and the return distribution.
  5. Examples — four tech stocks; five DJIA stocks, M–V vs. B–L.

Why go beyond Markowitz?

MPT assumes everyone shares the same expectations of returns, risks and correlations. Then a single market portfolio is optimal for everyone, and with common knowledge of rationality nobody trades (the no-trade theorem, Milgrom and Stokey 1982). In reality: bounded rationality, information asymmetries, heterogeneous views.

Classical MPT Black–Litterman
Homogeneous expectations Heterogeneous views allowed
One optimal portfolio (the market) Personalised allocations
Purely historical \((\mu, \Sigma)\) Equilibrium + subjective views
Sensitive to estimation error Bayesian updating of beliefs
Extreme, unintuitive weights More stable, diversified weights

Views come from fundamental analysis, dividend discount models, VaR and volatility models, analysts' estimates.

Market equilibrium

\(N\) assets with excess returns \(\mathbf r \sim N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)\). The key assumption: \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is unobservable, a latent random variable centred on the equilibrium premia:

\[\boldsymbol\mu \sim (\Pi, \tau\Sigma) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \boldsymbol\mu = \Pi + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \quad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim (0, \tau\Sigma)\]

Reverse optimisation

If everybody shared the same views, demand would equal supply. A mean–variance investor solves

\[\max_{\mathbf w}\; U = \mathbf w'\Pi - \tfrac12\gamma\,\mathbf w'\Sigma\mathbf w \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbf w^* = (\gamma\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi\]

Markowitz goes from returns to weights. B–L goes backwards: set \(\mathbf w^* = \mathbf w_{mkt}\) (observed market-cap weights) with the market risk aversion \(\gamma_{mkt}\) and read off the returns that make the market portfolio optimal:

\[\boxed{\Pi = \gamma_{mkt}\,\Sigma\,\mathbf w_{mkt}}\]

Under the CAPM (\(\alpha = 0\)) this is \(\Pi = \boldsymbol\beta\big(E(r_{mkt}) - r_f\big)\).

The role of \(\tau\)

\(\tau\) scales the uncertainty of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) around \(\Pi\): how much the equilibrium is trusted.

  • Small \(\tau\): strong confidence in equilibrium; views move returns little.
  • Large \(\tau\): less confidence; more room for views.
  • Literature: unspecified (Black–Litterman 1992); \(\tau = 1/T\) (Litterman and He, 1999, the common choice); \(\tau = 1\) (Meucci, 2010); estimated (Allaj, 2013).

The investor's views

\(k \le N\) views on expected excess returns:

\[\mathbf P\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf Q + \boldsymbol\eta, \qquad \boldsymbol\eta \sim N(0, \boldsymbol\Omega)\]

  • \(\mathbf Q\) (\(k \times 1\)): the view returns.
  • \(\mathbf P\) (\(k \times N\)): the pick matrix, linking views to assets.
  • \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) (\(k \times k\)): the uncertainty of the views.
Relative view Absolute view
One asset vs. another Return of a single asset
Row of \(\mathbf P\) sums to 0 Row of \(\mathbf P\) sums to 1
"Asset 1 beats asset 2 by 1%" "Asset 3 earns 1.5%"

Example (4 assets): v1 = asset 1 outperforms asset 2 by 1%, v2 = asset 3 earns 1.5%:

\[\mathbf P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1.0\% \\ 1.5\% \end{pmatrix}\]

Views can be weighted: the row \((1, -0.25, -0.75, 0)\) says asset 1 beats a 25/75 mix of assets 2 and 3.

The uncertainty \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). Black–Litterman take it diagonal (independent views). He and Litterman (1999, 2002) set it proportional to the variance of the view portfolios,

\[\boldsymbol\Omega = \mathbf P(\tau\Sigma)\mathbf P' \quad\text{(diagonal part: } \omega_{jj} = \tau\,\mathbf p_j\Sigma\mathbf p_j'\text{)}\]

Idzorek (2007) instead lets the investor state a confidence from 0% to 100%.

The B–L master formula

Two sources of information on \(\boldsymbol\mu\), assumed independent:

\[\begin{cases} \boldsymbol\mu = \Pi + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, & \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim (0, \tau\Sigma) \quad\text{(market equilibrium: prior)} \\ \mathbf P\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf Q + \boldsymbol\eta, & \boldsymbol\eta \sim (0, \boldsymbol\Omega) \quad\text{(investor's views: likelihood)} \end{cases}\]

Bayes: \(P(\boldsymbol\mu \mid \mathbf Q) \propto P(\mathbf Q \mid \boldsymbol\mu)\,P(\boldsymbol\mu)\). The posterior is \(N(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}, \bar M^{-1})\) with

\[\boxed{\bar{\boldsymbol\mu} = \big[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf P\big]^{-1}\big[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf Q\big], \qquad \bar M^{-1} = \big[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf P\big]^{-1}}\]

\(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}\) is a precision-weighted average of the equilibrium \(\Pi\) and the views \(\mathbf Q\): the more confident the investor (small \(\boldsymbol\Omega\)), the more the posterior tilts towards \(\mathbf Q\).

An equivalent, easier form (one matrix of size \(k\) to invert):

\[\bar{\boldsymbol\mu} = \Pi + \tau\Sigma\mathbf P'\big(\mathbf P\tau\Sigma\mathbf P' + \boldsymbol\Omega\big)^{-1}(\mathbf Q - \mathbf P\Pi)\]

The correction is proportional to the surprise \(\mathbf Q - \mathbf P\Pi\): a view equal to what equilibrium already implies changes nothing.

Derivation sketch. Multiply the two Gaussian densities and collect the terms in \(\boldsymbol\mu\):

\[P(\boldsymbol\mu \mid \mathbf Q) \propto \exp\Big[-\tfrac12\big(\boldsymbol\mu'A\boldsymbol\mu - 2\boldsymbol\mu'B + C\big)\Big], \quad A = (\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf P, \quad B = (\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf Q\]

Completing the square, \(\propto \exp[-\tfrac12(\boldsymbol\mu - A^{-1}B)'A(\boldsymbol\mu - A^{-1}B)]\), so \(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu} = A^{-1}B\) (posterior mean) and \(\bar M^{-1} = A^{-1}\) (posterior covariance). \(A\) is the posterior precision, \(B\) the information vector.

The distribution of returns

\(\boldsymbol\mu\) is uncertain, so returns carry extra variance:

\[\mathbf r \sim N(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}, \bar\Sigma), \qquad \bar\Sigma = \Sigma + \bar M^{-1}\]

\((\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}, \bar\Sigma)\) are the inputs of the usual mean–variance optimisation.

No confidence in the views, \(\boldsymbol\Omega \to \infty\): \(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu} \to \Pi\) and \(\bar\Sigma \to (1 + \tau)\Sigma\). The model falls back on the market equilibrium — a built-in safety net.

Try it: three assets, one relative view "A beats C by \(q\)". At 0% confidence the posterior sits on the equilibrium and the weights equal the market's; raising confidence pulls \(\bar\mu_A - \bar\mu_C\) towards \(q\), and the weights move only along the view (more A, less C). Set \(q\) equal to the equilibrium value and nothing moves. (Weights here use \((\gamma\Sigma)^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}\), i.e. \(\Sigma\) instead of \(\bar\Sigma\), to keep it simple.)

Numerical example: four tech stocks

Apple (A), Banana (B), Cherry (C), Date (D). \(r_M = 5\%\), \(\sigma_M^2 = 2.25\%\), \(r_f = 1\%\). With CAPM betas, \(\Pi = \boldsymbol\beta(5\% - 1\%)\):

A B C D
\(\hat\beta\) 1.10 1.05 1.01 1.50
\(\hat\Pi\) (%) 4.40 4.20 4.04 6.00
\(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}\) (%) 5.92 4.66 3.85 6.70

Views: v1 "A outperforms C by 5%" (relative), v2 "D yields 7%" (absolute):

\[\mathbf P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf Q = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tau = 1/20, \quad \boldsymbol\Omega = \mathbf P(\tau\Sigma)\mathbf P'\]

The views tilt returns: A rises from 4.40 to 5.92, C falls slightly to 3.85 (A − C goes from 0.36 to 2.07, part of the way to 5); D moves from 6.00 towards the bullish 7. B moves too (4.66), only through its correlation with the others.

Practice: five DJIA stocks

PG, MMM, IBM, MRK, AXP, Jan 2000 – Dec 2022 (275 monthly obs.), \(\gamma_{mkt} = 2.5\), \(\tau = 1/T\).

M–V vs. B–L frontier. The M–V frontier uses historical \((\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)\) only; the B–L frontier uses \((\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}, \bar\Sigma)\) and reflects the investor's views. Do not compare them in terms of dominance: different views give different frontiers.

Key takeaways

  • B–L starts from equilibrium: reverse optimisation \(\Pi = \gamma_{mkt}\Sigma\mathbf w_{mkt}\); \(\boldsymbol\mu \sim (\Pi, \tau\Sigma)\).
  • Views: \(\mathbf P\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf Q + \boldsymbol\eta\), \(\boldsymbol\eta \sim N(0, \boldsymbol\Omega)\); relative rows sum to 0, absolute to 1.
  • Master formula: \(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu} = [(\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf P]^{-1}[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi + \mathbf P'\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf Q]\) — a precision-weighted average; \(\bar\Sigma = \Sigma + \bar M^{-1}\).
  • \(\boldsymbol\Omega \to \infty\) ⇒ back to equilibrium; small \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) ⇒ tilt towards the views.
  • Result: more stable, diversified, intuitive weights that move only where the investor has views.
