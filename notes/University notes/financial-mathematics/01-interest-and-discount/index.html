Interest, Discount and Financial Laws

Lectures 1–2 of Financial Mathematics (University of Brescia). Money has a time value: one euro today is not one euro in a year. This note fixes the basic quantities and the functions that move money through time.

Roadmap:

  1. Financial operations — simple and complex.
  2. Accumulation and discounting — interest, discount and their rates.
  3. Accumulation and discount factors — the axioms and the link between \(i\) and \(d\).
  4. Force of interest — the instantaneous growth rate.
  5. Separable laws — when splitting an investment changes nothing.

Financial operations

A financial operation exchanges amounts of money that are certain in size but available at different dates. Time is the key variable.

  • Simple operation: two amounts only.
  • Complex operation: more than two amounts.

Accumulation and interest

In accumulation an agent gives up money today to receive a larger sum later.

  • \(C\): initial capital (present value), available at \(t_0\).
  • \(M\): final capital (accumulated value, montante), available at \(t\).
  • \(I\): interest, the reward for giving up the capital.

\[I = M - C \implies M = C + I\]

The interest rate is interest per unit of initial capital:

\[\text{interest rate} = \frac{I}{C} = \frac{M - C}{C}\]

Discounting

Discounting is the mirror operation: an agent wants today a sum due in the future, and pays a reward to whoever advances it.

  • \(D\): discount, the reward for the advance.
  • \(C\): discounted value.

\[M - D = C \implies D = M - C\]

The discount rate is discount per unit of final capital:

\[\text{discount rate} = \frac{D}{M} = \frac{M - C}{M}\]

So the same gap \(M - C\) is called interest (seen from \(C\)) or discount (seen from \(M\)). The rates differ because the denominators differ.

The accumulation factor

The accumulated value is a function of time and initial capital:

\[M(t) = C \cdot f(t)\]

\(f(t)\) is the accumulation factor (unit capital). On \(t \in [0, T)\), with \(T\) possibly infinite, \(f\) is acceptable only if:

  1. Positive: \(f(t) > 0\) for every \(t\).
  2. Starts at one: \(f(0) = 1\).
  3. Non-decreasing: if \(f\) is differentiable, \(f'(t) \ge 0\).

The interest rate for duration \(t\) is

\[i(t) = f(t) - 1\]

At \(t = 1\) we get the unit interest rate \(i\), the interest earned by one unit of capital in one period:

\[f(1) = 1 + i\]

The discount factor

Inverting \(M = C f(t)\):

\[C = \frac{M}{f(t)} = M \cdot v(t)\]

\(v(t)\) is the discount factor, the reciprocal (conjugate) of \(f\):

\[f(t) \cdot v(t) = 1\]

On \(t \in [0, T)\), \(v\) is acceptable only if:

  1. Positive: \(v(t) > 0\).
  2. Starts at one: \(v(0) = 1\).
  3. Non-increasing: if differentiable,

\[v'(t) = -\frac{f'(t)}{[f(t)]^2} \le 0\]

The discount rate for duration \(t\) and the unit discount rate are

\[d(t) = 1 - v(t), \qquad d = 1 - v(1)\]

Interest rate vs. discount rate

We know \(v(1) = 1 - d\) and, by conjugacy, \(v(1) = 1/(1+i)\). Equate and solve:

\[d = \frac{i}{1+i}, \qquad i = \frac{d}{1-d}\]

For any \(i > 0\) the denominator exceeds one, so \(d < i\) always.

Force of interest

Take a differentiable \(f\). In a very short interval \(dt\) the capital grows by about \(f'(t) dt\). Per unit of capital already accumulated, this is the force of interest (instantaneous intensity):

\[\delta(t) = \frac{f'(t)}{f(t)} = [\ln f(t)]'\]

\(\delta(t)\) is the instantaneous growth rate of capital. Integrate from \(0\) to \(t\) and use \(f(0) = 1\):

\[f(t) = e^{\int_0^t \delta(s) ds}\]

So a financial law can be given by \(f\), by \(v\), or by \(\delta\): each one determines the others.

Separable laws

A law is separable if splitting an investment into consecutive periods gives the same final amount as one single investment:

\[f(t) \cdot f(h) = f(t+h)\]

Theorem. A law is separable if and only if its force of interest is constant, that is, if and only if

\[f(t) = e^{\delta t}, \qquad \delta \ge 0\]

With a non-separable law, an investor could close the position and reopen it at once and end with a different amount. Note 2 shows that compound interest is separable and simple interest is not.

Key takeaways

  • \(I = M - C\) and interest rate \(I/C\); \(D = M - C\) and discount rate \(D/M\).
  • \(M = C f(t)\), \(C = M v(t)\), \(f(t) v(t) = 1\).
  • \(f\): positive, \(f(0) = 1\), non-decreasing. \(v\): positive, \(v(0) = 1\), non-increasing.
  • \(i = f(1) - 1\), \(d = 1 - v(1)\), \(d = i/(1+i) < i\).
  • Force of interest \(\delta(t) = f'(t)/f(t)\), and \(f(t) = e^{\int_0^t \delta(s) ds}\).
  • Separable \(\iff\) constant \(\delta\) \(\iff\) \(f(t) = e^{\delta t}\).
